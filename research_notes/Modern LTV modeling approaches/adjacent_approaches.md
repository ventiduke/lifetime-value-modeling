# Adjacent approaches to mainstream LTV prediction: survival, point processes, foundation models, uncertainty, lift measurement (as of 2026-11-18)

Provenance tags used on every finding: **[FT]** = full text read (arXiv HTML fetched in this session); **[ABS]** = only the arXiv abstract page was read; **[SNIP]** = only a web-search snippet was seen (publisher, blog and conference hosts were unreachable), so treat numbers as unverified. ## 1. Survival analysis and deep survival for churn and return time (Cox, RSF, DeepSurv/DeepHit, WTTE-RNN, recurrent-event models, discrete-time hazard GBDT). Are they used for LTV, and do they add anything over predicting weekly activity probabilities directly?

### Takeaway
On a weekly panel, the survival model that fits is a discrete-time hazard fitted on person-week rows, and that is a weekly-activity-probability model under another name: it can be run inside LightGBM (candidate A) and, once returns are allowed, it becomes the transition layer of a Markov/HMM model (candidate B). The evidence found does not show survival-specific architectures (RSF, DeepHit, WTTE-RNN) beating a boosted or linear person-period hazard; what survival framing adds is censoring handling (use of cohorts whose 52-week label has not matured), an explicit time-since-last-active (duration) effect, and coherent derived quantities.

### Cited Findings
- [ABS] "Survival stacking" recasts right-censored survival as binary classification on a stacked person-period data frame; with logistic regression it is approximately equivalent to the Cox proportional-hazards model, and it lets any classifier (random forests, gradient boosting, neural nets) be used for survival — [Craig, Zhong, Tibshirani 2021, arXiv 2107.13480](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.13480)
- [SNIP] "A discrete-time hazard model is just a logistic regression on person-period data", one row per subscriber per period at risk, with time-varying covariates and conversion of hazards to retention curves — [MetricGate tutorial](https://metricgate.com/blogs/churn-survival-discrete-hazard-r/) (vendor tutorial, simulated example only)
- [SNIP] A 2022 BMC Medical Research Methodology paper (Suresh, Severn, Ghosh) reports random survival forests improved on Cox but were "sometimes outperformed by the discrete-time approaches" — [PMC9316420](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9316420/)
- [FT] A 2026 benchmark of 14 tuned survival models on weekly dropout (OULAD learning-analytics data) found that in the weekly person-period family all five models (linear discrete-time hazard, neural discrete-time survival, gradient-boosted weekly hazard, CatBoost weekly hazard, Poisson piecewise-exponential) sat inside a 0.0011 integrated-Brier-score band (0.1399–0.1410); in the static early-window family Random Survival Forest led (time-dependent concordance 0.674) and XGBoost AFT was the sole outlier (IBS 0.150, concordance 0.577). Bootstrap resampling "qualifies these positions as directional signals, not claims of strict superiority". Inverse-frequency class weighting made calibration worse (IBS 0.44–0.52 vs 0.14 unweighted) — [arXiv 2604.08870](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.08870)
- [ABS] For neural discrete-time survival, parameterising the discrete hazard performs "slightly better" than parameterising the event-time PMF (the DeepHit-style choice) in simulations and real data — [Kvamme & Borgan 2019, arXiv 1910.06724](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06724)
- [ABS] Deep Recurrent Survival Analysis (DRSA) models the conditional event probability per time step with an RNN and no distributional assumption, claiming significant gains over prior survival methods on three real-world tasks — [Ren et al. 2018, arXiv 1809.02403](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02403)
- [SNIP] WTTE-RNN (Martinsson, Chalmers master's thesis, 2016/2017) has an RNN emit the two parameters of a discrete or continuous Weibull distribution over time-to-next-event, trained with a censored log-likelihood; proportional hazards and Weibull AFT are special cases; the thesis's own evaluation used simulated data with varying censoring and temporal resolution; churn is listed as a target application — [Chalmers thesis PDF](https://publications.lib.chalmers.se/records/fulltext/253611/253611.pdf)
- [FT] Games-industry survival work: conditional inference survival ensembles on the mobile game Age of Ishtaria, trained on n = 2,500 high-value players ("whales"), improved on Cox regression (peak bootstrap cross-validated prediction error 0.20 for the ensemble vs 0.21 for Cox; evaluated with integrated Brier score). Churn was defined as 10 days of inactivity, justified by the finding that whales' purchases after returning from a 10-day gap were only 1.4% of that group's revenue — [Periáñez et al. 2016, arXiv 1710.02264](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.02264)
- [SNIP] Follow-up work from the same group used conditional inference survival ensembles and random survival forests to predict churn level/playtime and time-to-first-purchase (conversion), not lifetime value — [Bertens, Guitart, Periáñez, arXiv 1710.02262](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1710.02262); [Guitart et al. 2019, arXiv 1906.10320](https://web3.arxiv.org/pdf/1906.10320)
- [ABS] Game Data Mining Competition (NCSOFT Blade & Soul logs, ~100 GB from 10,000 players; tracks for churn classification and survival time, across a subscription-to-free-to-play business model change): "highly ranked competitors used deep learning, tree boosting, and linear regression" — no single family dominated — [Lee et al. 2018, arXiv 1802.02301](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.02301)
- [FT] For LTV proper in a game, the comparison in the literature is deep nets versus parametric buy-till-you-die models rather than versus survival models: CNN/DNN on daily behaviour logs beat Pareto/NBD-family models at a 365-day horizon, with errors on the top-20% spenders "roughly half as large as in parametric models"; the parametric models wrongly predict no purchases for players who keep spending and systematically underestimate top spenders — [Chen et al. 2018, arXiv 1811.12799](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12799)
- [ABS] A 2026 study of customer-return timing (Amazon, Taobao, RetailRocket, plus a Thumbtack marketplace dataset) reports returns are "near-memoryless": only "a single-digit percentage of gap variance" is point-predictable from any covariate, and the inter-event decay clock is "nearly sufficient" while added conditioning signals (LTV, category, recency/frequency, calendar, geography) are "redundant or harmful on top of it" — [arXiv 2608.11555](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11555)
- [SNIP] Gradient boosting remains the reference for fixed-horizon tabular churn; one consultancy's best churn model was "a binary classification model for each month using gradient-boosted decision trees"; an Annals of Operations Research (2023) paper found recurrent networks suit churn but found no statistical evidence that hybrid approaches improve further over conventional classifiers — [Grid Dynamics](https://www.griddynamics.com/?p=4237); [Springer s10479-023-05259-9](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10479-023-05259-9)

### Inferences
- A weekly discrete-time hazard with boosting is not a new candidate: it is LightGBM (A) trained on person-week rows with "weeks since last active" as a feature and a logistic loss. With both a churn hazard (active to inactive) and a return hazard (inactive to active), it is a two-state covariate-driven Markov chain, i.e. the observed-state special case of the HMM (B). It is best treated as a variant inside A or B, not as a separate bake-off slot.
- What the survival framing buys: (i) censoring — cohorts whose 52-week window has not closed can still contribute every observed person-week, so the training data can be up to a year fresher than a direct 52-week-label model; (ii) known-in-advance time-varying covariates (the season calendar) enter the hazard for each future week naturally; (iii) P(any active week in 52) and expected active weeks come from one set of weekly hazards and are therefore mutually consistent.
- What it costs: with recurrent churn-and-return, expected active weeks is no longer a closed-form function of one survival curve; it needs a 52-step forward recursion over the active/inactive state (cheap, two states) or simulation. Hours and spend still need a separate conditional-amount model.
- Single-event survival models (Cox, RSF, DeepSurv, DeepHit, WTTE-RNN as usually applied) assume churn is absorbing. That assumption was defensible in the Periáñez whale data (returns worth 1.4% of revenue) but is the opposite of this product, where season launches drive returns. They would have to be applied as alternating-state or recurrent-event models to be usable.
- Random survival forests and conditional inference ensembles in the cited game studies were trained on thousands of players; nothing found shows them run at 100M scale, and kernel/forest survival predictions (a survival curve per user per tree) are heavier to score than a single boosted hazard.
- The "near-memoryless returns" result, if it transfers, predicts that time-since-last-active plus the calendar carries most of the timing signal, which favours simple hazard features in A over elaborate deep survival heads.

### Gaps
- No source found that directly compares a survival/hazard formulation against direct weekly-activity or direct 52-week-count prediction for user-level LTV; the question "does it add anything" is answered here by structural argument, not by a benchmark.
- No head-to-head of WTTE-RNN versus gradient boosting on return time was found. A search snippet mentioned a telecom churn study in which an MLP beat WTTE-RNN on AUC, precision and recall, but the source could not be identified, so it is not cited.
- DeepSurv and DeepHit were not individually researched beyond the Kvamme comparison; no evidence on them for consumer churn was found.
- No published recurrent-event (Andersen–Gill, PWP) boosting application to consumer return behaviour was found.
- The Chalmers WTTE-RNN thesis and the BMC paper could not be opened; their details are snippet-only.

## 2. Temporal point processes and neural TPPs (Hawkes, RMTPP, Transformer Hawkes) for user activity and purchase timing: evidence versus RNN/GBDT on long-horizon user-level counts, and cost at scale

### Takeaway
Neural TPPs are not a credible bake-off candidate here: the one benchmark built for long-horizon event forecasting finds simple statistical baselines win or tie in 6 of 10 tasks, the models are tuned for next-event prediction, autoregressive rollout is slow, and the data in this project is already aggregated to weeks, which removes the continuous timestamps that are a TPP's only structural advantage.

### Cited Findings
- [FT] HoTPP (Karpukhin, Shipilov, Savchenko), the first benchmark for long-horizon marked-TPP forecasting, evaluated statistical baselines (MostPopular, HistoryDensity), intensity-based RNN/continuous-time models (RMTPP, NHP, AttNHP, ODE), intensity-free IFTPP, HYPRO reranking and a Diffusion model. "In 6 out of 10 long-horizon tasks, simple statistical baselines either outperform or match modern MTPP models": the statistical baselines rank first in 3 of 10 long-horizon tasks and are statistically tied with the top methods in 3 more — [arXiv 2406.14341](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.14341)
- [FT] Same paper: "methods excelling at next-event prediction often underperform in long-horizon scenarios, and vice versa"; "next-event and horizon prediction are distinct tasks requiring specialized models, and none of the existing approaches achieve top performance in both" — [arXiv 2406.14341](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.14341)
- [FT] Same paper: most autoregressive methods show mode collapse (low entropy of predicted labels on all datasets except MIMIC-IV); predicted sequence length strongly affects quality — [arXiv 2406.14341](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.14341)
- [FT] Cost reported by HoTPP: "Some methods are particularly slow, especially during autoregressive inference on large datasets. Straightforward evaluation can take several hours on a single Nvidia V100 GPU"; the benchmark's largest datasets have "up to tens of millions of events"; training ranged from 5 minutes (RMTPP, StackOverflow) to 15 hours (NHP, Transactions); one method (NJDTPP) took two hours for a single test-set prediction on the smallest dataset and ContiFormer could not be run at all — [arXiv 2406.14341](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.14341)
- [ABS] The standard review of neural TPPs lists open challenges and applications but frames TPPs as "probabilistic generative models for continuous-time event sequences" — [Shchur et al. 2021, arXiv 2104.03528](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.03528)
- [ABS] EasyTPP notes that before 2023 there was "no central benchmark" for neural TPPs, impeding comparison and reproduction — [Xue et al. 2023, arXiv 2307.08097](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.08097)
- [ABS] Customer-return timing study: added covariate conditioning on a TPP is "statistically null on the public benchmarks, at most 0.06 NLL; null to mildly harmful on the marketplace"; the authors also report "read-out/leakage pitfalls we hit and retracted" in TPP evaluation — [arXiv 2608.11555](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11555)
- [ABS] Discrete-time renewal processes generalise Croston-type intermittent-demand models, allow an RNN to replace exponential smoothing, and extend "trivially" to continuous-time TPPs when individual order timestamps are available — the link between weekly intermittent series and TPPs — [Türkmen, Januschowski, Wang, Cemgil 2020, arXiv 2010.01550](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.01550)
- [SNIP] A multivariate periodic Hawkes process beat a periodic Poisson process and LDA at predicting purchase event types in a short window on digital purchase traces; this is not a CLV or long-horizon count benchmark — [arXiv 2005.06542](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.06542)
- [SNIP] In the BTYD literature, models with quite different assumptions "tend to have a similar predictive performance"; a regularity extension (Pareto/GGG) improves customer-level predictions when purchase timing is regular — [WU Vienna, "Ticking Away the Moments"](https://research.wu.ac.at/en/publications/ticking-away-the-moments-timing-regularity-helps-to-better-predic-7/)

### Inferences
- A TPP's value is modelling irregular continuous inter-event times. The project's panel is weekly counts and sums (active flag, hours, spend), so a TPP would need the raw session/purchase event log, which multiplies data volume and gives no evidence-backed accuracy gain for 52-week totals.
- A 52-week horizon means rolling out many events per user by sampling; HoTPP's "several hours on a V100" for tens of millions of events implies that 100M users with multi-event rollouts is far outside a 30-minute budget without a very large GPU fleet. This is an order-of-magnitude argument, not a measured figure.
- The useful TPP idea to borrow is the decay clock: exponentially decayed recency/intensity features (Hawkes-style kernels over past weeks) as inputs to LightGBM or the sequence model.

### Gaps
- No study found that compares a neural TPP (or Hawkes model) with GBDT or with an RNN trained directly on horizon counts for user-level long-horizon engagement or spend; no neural-TPP-versus-Pareto/NBD CLV benchmark was found either.
- No published per-user inference throughput for neural TPPs at 10M+ user scale was found.
- HoTPP's table values (T-mAP, OTD per dataset) were not transcribed; only the paper's prose summary is cited.

## 3. Time-series foundation models (Chronos, TimesFM, Moirai, Lag-Llama, TimeGPT) and tabular foundation models (TabPFN, TabICL, CARTE, TabDPT), 2023–2026: applicability to tens of millions of short, sparse, intermittent per-user series; intermittent-demand benchmarks; inference cost; row limits

### Takeaway
Neither family earns a production slot. Zero-shot time-series foundation models on intermittent series behave roughly like the empirical distribution of each series' own history and cost one to three orders of magnitude too much to score 100M users in 30 minutes; tabular foundation models have reached about 1M context rows by late 2026 but their own papers concede they trail tuned conventional models on temporal splits and large tables, which is exactly this problem. Two cheap uses survive: a tabular foundation model as a sample-efficiency reference on a subsample (or distilled), and a zero-shot TSFM as an extra "persistence-class" baseline on a small user sample.

### Cited Findings
**Time-series foundation models — capability and cost**
- [FT] Chronos-2 (120M parameters; a 28M variant exists) handles univariate, multivariate and covariate-informed forecasting zero-shot via group attention; context up to 8,192 steps; reported throughput "300 time series per second" on a single NVIDIA A10G, measured on a batch of 1,024 series with context length 2,048 and prediction length 64 — [Ansari et al. 2025, arXiv 2510.15821](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15821)
- [SNIP] On fev-bench Chronos-2 reports a 90.7% average win rate and 47.3% skill score versus TiRex at 80.8% and 42.6%, and on the covariate subset it outperforms baselines including TabPFN-TS (authors' own numbers) — [arXiv 2510.15821](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.15821)
- [FT] The Chronos-2 paper itself points to "small-data or cold-start scenarios" as a future application of grouping series by metadata or embeddings, i.e. not a demonstrated strength — [arXiv 2510.15821](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15821)
- [FT] TimesFM-3 timing in an independent study: 24.9 ms per series batched on a consumer GPU (GTX 1660 Ti) and 47.1 ms on CPU (12 threads), on 200 seasonal series of length 96, against 2.8 ms for seasonal naive and 9.1 ms for Theta per series on one core — [arXiv 2610.00589](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00589)
- [FT] TimesFM-3's weights are released under a non-commercial licence ("solely for your Non-Commercial Purposes"); "for a commercial application, TimesFM-3 is not licensed as released, whatever its accuracy" — [arXiv 2610.00589](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00589)
- [FT] In a 25-method production-panel benchmark, Chronos T5-Large "takes roughly fifty times longer per series than any other foundation model here" — [arXiv 2609.27867](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27867)

**Time-series foundation models — intermittent / sparse series**
- [FT] Pre-registered simulation study (7,200 series, nine known processes): on an intermittent process with about 70% zeros, TimesFM-3's MASE advantage "is thus the advantage of a forecast close to zero" — the all-zero forecast has the lowest MASE at every length (0.668–0.718 vs 0.683–0.780 for TimesFM-3's median). Using the mean of its deciles, TimesFM-3's RMSSE (0.694–0.760) is "level with the best Croston-family method and with the mean of the history (0.692 to 0.744)". Its pinball loss (0.288–0.325) is slightly worse than the empirical deciles of the history (0.282–0.314). Conclusion: "TimesFM-3 shows no advantage over simple benchmarks: it behaves much like the empirical distribution of the history, and so do the other foundation models" (Chronos-Bolt, Chronos-2, TiRex, TimesFM-2.5 were in the roster). A purpose-built model (iETS) had lower pinball loss than TimesFM-3 at every length — [arXiv 2610.00589](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00589)
- [FT] Same study: foundation-model robustness "holds only at short horizons"; at steps 25–48 the three foundation models tested forecast a decline on a saturating process whose level stays flat; 80% intervals of both families under-cover, "TimesFM-3 more at longer horizons" — [arXiv 2610.00589](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00589)
- [ABS] EXAONE Demand's motivation: in TSFM pretraining corpora "demand series make up only a small fraction" and demand data has "short histories, frequent zeros, censoring by stock-outs, and exogenous events that the series does not record"; a demand-specific corpus (11.3M series) and adapter are claimed to beat 36 TSFMs on 22 held-out datasets (authors' claim) — [arXiv 2609.30880](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30880)
- [SNIP] A NeurIPS 2026 paper testing six open-weight probabilistic TSFMs against Croston-SBA under sparse demand: 6 of 10 comparisons favour the TSFM when replanning is free, but with a fixed plan-change cost (κ = 0.25) all ten significantly favour Croston-SBA — forecast instability across origins is the cost — [NeurIPS 2026 virtual page](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/175006)
- [SNIP] An anonymous 2026 draft reports D-Linear with a negative-binomial head beating zero-shot Chronos and Moirai on intermittent demand and argues the distribution head matters more than the backbone (low reliability: unreviewed draft) — [draft PDF](https://idrori.github.io/open/problems/ML/llm-forecasting-models-intermittent-time-series-comparison/paper/main.pdf)
- [SNIP] A dissertation comparing Chronos, TimeGPT, Lag-Llama, TimesFM and Uni2TS on M5 found TimesFM consistently best of those in zero-shot and fine-tuned settings (no comparison with the LightGBM winners in the snippet) — [IPP repository](https://recipp.ipp.pt/bitstreams/e35ca683-7332-46b7-9a07-42044fa40a59/download)
- [FT] Production marketplace panel (1,887 business customers, 67 months, 24 methods incl. six 2025-era TSFMs): "foundation models and global neural models occupy the top of the table" per customer, but intermittent-demand methods win on the intermittent class, three different methods lead the four demand classes, and the ranking depends on the evaluation design — [arXiv 2609.27867](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27867)
- [ABS] General benchmarks have moved to leak-free zero-shot evaluation (TIME: 50 fresh datasets, 98 tasks, 12 TSFMs), but none of those found targets per-user engagement series — [arXiv 2602.12147](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12147)
- [ABS] A cost-aware industrial study concludes "TSFMs are task-dependent deployment options rather than default replacements for fitted lightweight models" — [arXiv 2608.22968](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22968)

**Tabular foundation models — limits and cost**
- [FT] Recommended maximum training rows / features by TabPFN release: v1 1,000 / 100; v2 10,000 / 500; 2.5 and 2.6 100,000 / 2,000; TabPFN-3 1,000,000 / 2,000; TabPFN-3.5 1,000,000 / 6,000 (220M parameters; "Fast" variant 84M). These are "recommended limits for strong performance, not computational limits". Runtime scales quadratically in training rows above 128k rows — [TabPFN-3.5 technical report, arXiv 2609.17895](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17895)
- [ABS] TabPFN-2.5 abstract states it is "built for datasets with up to 50,000 data points and 2,000 features" and claims a 100% win rate against default XGBoost on classification datasets up to 10,000 rows and 87% up to 100K rows (85% for regression) — [arXiv 2511.08667](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08667) (note the 50K figure in this abstract versus 100K in the later TabPFN-3.5 table)
- [FT] TabICLv2 keeps O(n² + nm²) complexity; with disk offloading it processes a table of 1M samples and 500 features (80% train / 20% test) on one H100 in 450 s using under 24 GB CPU RAM and 50 GB GPU memory, or 115 s with CPU offloading that needs 250 GB RAM; it is 10.6× faster than TabPFN-2.5 on an H100 at 50K samples; on ~600K-sample datasets it "still performs strongly" while TabPFN-2.5 ran out of memory — [Qu et al. 2026, arXiv 2602.11139](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11139)
- [FT] TabICLv2 limitations stated by its authors: "datasets with millions of samples remain challenging"; multi-output regression and handling distribution shifts "are also left to future work"; distributional regression is "not evaluated beyond toy datasets" — [arXiv 2602.11139](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11139)
- [FT] TabPFN-3.5 report, citing the BeyondArena benchmark (Purucker et al.): tabular foundation models "excel on clean i.i.d. data, yet even the best of them fall behind tuned conventional models on grouped or temporal splits, on tables with many rows or many features, and on tables with text or high-cardinality features". Even for TabPFN-3.5, "tuned and ensembled MLPs retain the highest performance on grouped, temporal, and large datasets", with parity claimed only on datasets up to 100K rows — [arXiv 2609.17895](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17895)
- [FT] Deployment paths offered: a KV cache over training rows so test rows are predicted without recomputing the context; a proprietary distillation engine that converts TabPFN-2.5 into a compact MLP or tree ensemble "preserving most of its accuracy" (benchmarked on datasets under 10k rows); TabPFN-3.5 weights are licensed for research and internal evaluation, with production use requiring a commercial enterprise licence — [arXiv 2609.17895](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17895); [arXiv 2511.08667](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08667)
- [FT] TabPFN-3.5 as a forecaster: the general tabular checkpoint (no time-series fine-tuning) beats the time-series-specific TabPFN-TS-3 on fev-bench; a search snippet of the same report places it sixth, behind TimesFM-3, two Chronos-2 variants, TiRex-2 and Toto-2.0 — [arXiv 2609.17895](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17895)

**Relational foundation models (adjacent, surfaced during search)**
- [FT] RelBench defines `user-churn` and `user-ltv` tasks (rel-amazon: churn = no review in next 3 months; LTV = dollar value of products bought and reviewed in next 3 months) with leakage-safe temporal seed times. On `user-ltv` test MAE, LightGBM on raw single-table features scored 16.783 (identical to predicting zero) versus 14.313 for the graph-based relational deep learning model; but that LightGBM baseline uses "only information from the single entity table". Against an experienced data scientist's hand-engineered LightGBM, relational deep learning won 11 of 15 tasks and was "struggling more on regression" — [Robinson et al. 2024, arXiv 2407.20060](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.20060)
- [SNIP] RelBench leaderboard values reported in search for `user-ltv` (lower is better; scale differs from the paper's table): raw-feature LightGBM 0.2919, data-scientist LightGBM 0.2422, KumoRFM fine-tuned 0.2474, KumoRFM-2 in-context 0.2421 — i.e. a carefully engineered LightGBM roughly matches the relational foundation model on LTV — [RelBench leaderboard](https://relbench-leaderboard.static.hf.space/index.html)

### Inferences
- Scoring arithmetic from the cited throughputs (assumes one series per user and the published benchmark settings; real throughput on ~100–260-step weekly contexts would differ and was not measured): Chronos-2 at 300 series/s needs about 93 GPU-hours for 100M series, i.e. roughly 185 A10G GPUs to finish in 30 minutes, per target series. TimesFM-3 at 24.9 ms per series needs about 690 hours on the consumer GPU used in that study, i.e. roughly 1,400 such GPUs for 30 minutes. A LightGBM or small GRU pass is orders of magnitude cheaper per user.
- In-context tabular models cannot hold the training set: 100M users × many weekly snapshots is 10⁹–10¹⁰ rows versus a 10⁶-row recommended context. They can only be used with a ≤1M-row subsample as context (then 100M test rows scored against a cached context — no published throughput at that scale) or distilled into a tree/MLP, at which point the production artefact is a GBDT again.
- Zero-shot TSFMs see one user's series at a time (Chronos-2's group attention aside), so they cannot learn the cross-user regularities that dominate LTV (cohort decay, country/platform effects, the response to a season launch for lapsed users). With a short, mostly-zero history, the intermittent-demand evidence says they return something close to the history's empirical distribution, which is the persistence baseline (R) at far higher cost.
- The season calendar is the main exogenous driver; of the TSFMs researched only Chronos-2 (and TabPFN-style tabular forecasters) accept known future covariates zero-shot, and no evidence was found on how well a zero-shot model learns "a lapsed user returns at a season launch" from a single user's own history.
- A fine-tuned TSFM backbone would effectively be candidate C (a sequence model trained on the panel) with a larger, pre-initialised network; its interest is as an initialisation ablation inside C, not as a separate approach.
- Recommended supporting uses only: (i) TabPFN-3.5 or TabICLv2 fitted on a ≤1M-row stratified sample of the RFM feature table as a "how much is LightGBM leaving on the table" reference for each target; (ii) Chronos-2 zero-shot on a 100K-user sample as an additional naive-class baseline. Licences must be checked before either leaves evaluation.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or benchmark study was found that tests any TSFM, zero-shot or fine-tuned, on per-user engagement or LTV series. One blog (dypsis-ai) shows a churn table comparing LightGBM, Cox, TimesFM 2.5 and Chronos 2 with no dataset or method; it was not used.
- No published throughput for Moirai 2.0, Lag-Llama, TimeGPT or TiRex was retrieved; Lag-Llama, TimeGPT and Moirai were not individually researched beyond appearing in the cited comparisons. A Manning book preview snippet says Lag-Llama is weaker on weekly/monthly data and horizons above 60 without fine-tuning (unverified).
- CARTE and TabDPT were not researched; no row-limit or cost figures for them are given here.
- GIFT-Eval and fev-bench leaderboards as of late 2026 were not retrieved directly; TabArena numbers are quoted only as reported by model authors.
- No measured cost for scoring 100M test rows with a cached-context tabular foundation model, and no evidence on distilled-TFM accuracy at more than 10k training rows.
- The BeyondArena paper (Purucker et al.) was not read; its finding is quoted via the TabPFN-3.5 report.

## 4. Intermittent-demand forecasting methods (Croston, TSB, DeepAR with negative-binomial or zero-inflated likelihoods, global models) as an analogy for sparse per-user weekly series: any transfer to user-level LTV?

### Takeaway
The transferable lessons are about likelihoods, global training and metrics, not about new model classes: use a global model with a zero-aware count/amount head (hurdle, negative binomial, Tweedie), prefer direct multi-horizon outputs to autoregressive sampling, and never score sparse series with median-eliciting metrics. Croston-type per-series smoothing is a per-user baseline in the persistence class, not a contender.

### Cited Findings
- [FT] First systematic local-versus-global comparison on intermittent series (five retail/supply-chain datasets, over 40,000 series; heads: negative binomial, hurdle-shifted negative binomial, Tweedie): "Among global models, TiDE, a simple neural network architecture, achieves the best accuracy; it also consistently outperforms local models and has lower computational requirements"; large global models (DeepAR, PatchTST, Autoformer) are "much more computationally demanding and less accurate"; "the Tweedie provides the best estimates of the highest quantiles" — [Damato, Azzimonti, Corani 2026, arXiv 2601.14031](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14031)
- [FT] Same paper on GBDT: gradient boosted trees "have good performances in producing point forecasts", but the probabilistic LightGBM variants were dropped from the analysis as "both more computationally demanding and less accurate", showed large variability across training runs, and LightGBM with a Tweedie distribution head failed numerically on M5 — [arXiv 2601.14031](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14031)
- [FT] Same paper: DeepAR "adopts autoregressive sampling to forecast h steps ahead. This strategy with such a large model is, however, computationally demanding", whereas TiDE, FNN and DLinear are multi-horizon architectures that directly output distribution parameters for all h steps — [arXiv 2601.14031](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14031)
- [FT] Same paper: the main finding is "the better performance of global models compared to local models on the upper part of the forecast distribution (quantiles 0.8, 0.9, 0.95)", while on RMSSE (a mean-type point metric) local methods beat DLinear on three of five datasets. "The results on M5 constitute an exception. The large number of series, their length, and the low volatility of the demand allows all global models (including some of those discarded …) to be generally stable and effective" — i.e. with a very large cross-section the differences between global model classes shrink — [arXiv 2601.14031](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14031)
- [SNIP] M5 "Accuracy" winners built on "multiple direct and recursive LightGBM models", and LightGBM-based ensembles outperformed deep learning entries including those from Amazon's forecasting team; LightGBM "has been part of successful submissions to the M5 competition … for instance when coupled with the Tweedie loss" (the last clause is [FT] from arXiv 2601.14031) — [PMC9232271](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9232271); [arXiv 2601.14031](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14031)
- [SNIP] Conflicting evidence: a separate M5 store-item study with Tweedie deviance loss found deep models beat classical and global ML methods and that "DeepAR-Direct offers the most favourable balance between accuracy and computational efficiency" — [arXiv 2506.05941](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05941v2)
- [ABS] DeepAR trains one autoregressive RNN across many related series and reports ~15% accuracy improvement over prior methods; its count-data likelihood is the negative binomial (from prior knowledge of the paper; only the abstract was re-read) — [Salinas et al. 2017, arXiv 1704.04110](https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04110)
- [ABS] Croston-type models are special cases of discrete-time renewal processes that can capture "aging, clustering and quasi-periodicity in demand arrivals", and exponential smoothing can be replaced by an RNN — [arXiv 2010.01550](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.01550)
- [FT] On a ~70%-zero process, MASE "is minimised by the conditional median, which is zero or near it", so the all-zero forecast wins; sMAPE "scores a zero forecast of a zero outcome as perfect" and "is not informative"; Croston-family methods have the better RMSSE; the empirical distribution of each series' history is a hard-to-beat probabilistic benchmark — [arXiv 2610.00589](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00589)
- [FT] On a production panel, the rank correlation between methods' point-accuracy ranking and interval-quality ranking is 0.39 overall and 0.02 on intermittent demand — the best point forecaster is not the best probabilistic forecaster where series are sparse — [arXiv 2609.27867](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27867)
- [ABS] The ZILN loss used by candidate A is the LTV community's own answer to the same zero-inflation plus heavy-tail problem (zero point mass plus lognormal) — [Wang, Liu, Miao 2019, arXiv 1912.07753](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.07753)
- [SNIP] Reported failure mode: "standard ZILN optimization frequently collapses under the extreme sparsity (>80% zeros)" in a B2B setting, motivating a focal variant (April 2026 preprint, unreviewed) — [VALOR, arXiv 2604.02472](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.02472v1)

### Inferences
- Structural differences from retail demand limit direct transfer: (i) the target here is a 52-week sum, where averaging over weeks removes much of the week-level intermittency; (ii) user "death" is common and partially reversible, unlike an SKU's demand rate; (iii) the cross-section (100M users) is vastly larger than the time dimension, so global models are the only sensible choice and per-series (local) methods such as Croston/TSB/iETS have nothing to pool.
- The Tweedie result suggests adding a Tweedie objective for expected hours and expected spend as an ablation next to ZILN in candidate A: Tweedie optimises the mean directly, has no separate zero head, and its deviance is a natural mean-eliciting score for zero-inflated positives. The reported numerical failure of a Tweedie *distribution head* in LightGBM (as opposed to the built-in Tweedie mean objective) is a caution for the distributional variant only.
- The "TiDE beats DeepAR/Transformers on sparse series" result is a reason to include a cheap feed-forward multi-horizon model (lag window plus static covariates into an MLP with distribution heads) as a low-cost arm inside C; it would also be the easiest neural model to fit into the 30-minute budget.
- A per-user Croston/TSB or "empirical distribution of own history" forecast belongs in baseline set R as the intermittent-demand analogue of persistence.

### Gaps
- No study found that applies Croston, TSB or iETS to user-level LTV, and no source was retrieved that describes TSB specifically.
- The M5 primary competition papers could not be opened; whether the winning solution used Tweedie loss is confirmed only indirectly.
- No evidence found on zero-inflated (as opposed to negative-binomial) DeepAR likelihoods for user-level data.

## 5. Generative / simulation approaches: simulate future weekly trajectories per user (autoregressive sequence models, state-space models, deep Markov models, neural HMMs) versus direct multi-horizon heads

### Takeaway
No head-to-head evidence shows per-user trajectory simulation beating direct horizon heads on long-horizon totals, and the adjacent evidence (event-sequence and intermittent-demand benchmarks) leans toward direct outputs on both accuracy and cost. Simulation's real value is coherence and full joint distributions; the cheap way to get that is an analytic forward recursion in a small-state model (the HMM already in the plan), not sampling a neural network 52 steps for 100M users.

### Cited Findings
- [FT] In long-horizon event forecasting, "autoregressive models can be suboptimal for long-horizon predictions"; autoregressive rollout "involves applying the model to its own potentially erroneous predictions"; methods built to predict the horizon jointly (HYPRO, Diffusion) do better on horizon metrics while autoregressive ones win next-event metrics — [HoTPP, arXiv 2406.14341](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.14341)
- [FT] On intermittent series, direct multi-horizon architectures (TiDE, DLinear, FNN) were more accurate than DeepAR, whose autoregressive sampling is "computationally demanding; the number of simulated trajectories should be decided by considering also the computational cost" — [arXiv 2601.14031](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14031)
- [SNIP] Classical econometric evidence points the other way for well-specified linear models: on 171 US monthly macro series, "iterated forecasts typically outperform the direct forecasts", increasingly so with horizon; the trade-off is that iteration is more efficient but "prone to bias if the one-step ahead model is misspecified", and direct forecasts may dominate under misspecification — [Marcellino, Stock, Watson 2006](https://www.princeton.edu/~mwatson/papers/hstep_3.pdf)
- [SNIP] The main simulation-style LTV model in the marketing literature: Valendin, Reutterer, Platzer, Kalcher, "Customer base analysis with recurrent neural networks" (IJRM 39(4), 2022) — an LSTM that learns autoregressively to predict each customer's transactions in the next period and is rolled forward; benchmarked against probability models including Pareto/GGG; keywords include seasonality and customer covariates; open-source implementation exists — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ijrema/v39y2022i4p988-1018.html); [EMAC 2020 abstract](https://proceedings.emac-online.org/pdfs/A2020-60350.pdf)
- [ABS] A 2026 multi-task Transformer on weekly transaction panels of 966 companies (firm-level, not user-level) forecasts customer-base primitives jointly and aligns them with revenue: total-sales error 30% below the best established customer-base benchmark, but only 2.65% below a Transformer forecasting total sales directly, a difference that "is not statistically significant (p=.222)"; a search snippet of the same paper says its LSTM benchmark (Valendin-style) generates holdout forecasts recursively — [arXiv 2608.02911](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02911)
- [ABS] CLVAE keeps the process-based likelihood of attrition–transaction–spend models but replaces the parametric heterogeneity distribution with a VAE latent; one model for attrition, transactions and spend; claims improvement over "the latest benchmarks" across datasets and horizons — [arXiv 2604.22636](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22636)
- [ABS] "Generative regression" (Kuaishou) is a different idea sometimes confused with trajectory simulation: it generates the digits/tokens of a single scalar target autoregressively to handle wide, imbalanced ranges; a search snippet says it was also tested on LTV — [arXiv 2412.20211](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.20211)
- [SNIP] Evidence that a hybrid is already standard in demand forecasting: M5's winning approach mixed "direct and recursive LightGBM models" — see section 4 source — [PMC9232271](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9232271)

### Inferences
- Cost arithmetic: direct heads need one forward pass per user (10⁸ evaluations). Sampling S paths of 52 weekly steps needs 52 × S × 10⁸ step evaluations — 5.2 × 10¹¹ for S = 100. At 30 minutes that is about 2.9 × 10⁸ step evaluations per second, feasible only for a very small recurrent cell on a large GPU fleet. Simulation at full population scale conflicts with the hard constraint; it is affordable on a sample (for calibration checks or scenario analysis) or by distilling simulated outputs into a direct model.
- A small discrete-state model gives the same coherent multi-target distribution without sampling: with K latent states, the 52-week distribution of active weeks and the expected hours/spend follow from K×K matrix products per user per week (K² × 52 operations), and the season calendar enters through the transition covariates. That is candidate B; "deep Markov model / neural HMM" is best read as B with a neural transition/emission network, i.e. a B–C hybrid ablation rather than a new approach.
- Simulation gives things direct heads cannot: (i) counterfactual calendars ("what if the season launch moves two weeks"), (ii) joint distributions across targets and across weeks, hence correct intervals for sums, (iii) forecasts for arbitrary windows, including the fixed-target windows needed for martingale-style forecast revisions (section 7). Direct heads fix the window at training time.
- Exposure bias (training on real histories, rolling out on own samples) is the main accuracy risk for neural rollouts over 52 steps, and HoTPP's mode-collapse finding suggests sampled trajectories under-represent rare behaviours such as returns after long lapses — the behaviour that matters most here.

### Gaps
- No user-level LTV study was found that compares simulated-trajectory aggregation against direct multi-horizon heads with the same backbone.
- The Valendin et al. paper could not be opened; whether and by how much the LSTM beat Pareto/NBD-type models, and how seasonality entered, is unverified.
- No sources were retrieved on deep Markov models or neural HMMs applied to customer journeys; nothing can be said about their empirical accuracy here.

## 6. Uncertainty: conformal prediction (including CQR) for heavy-tailed LTV, distributional GBDT (NGBoost, LightGBMLSS, XGBoostLSS, quantile regression forests), deep ensembles. What gives valid intervals cheaply at 100M scale?

### Takeaway
The cheapest route to defensible intervals at 100M scale is post-hoc: take quantiles from a model already in the plan (ZILN/lognormal quantiles or two extra LightGBM quantile models) and apply group-wise split-conformal correction (CQR) from a held-out matured cohort — a table lookup at scoring time. Its guarantee is marginal and assumes exchangeability, and the evidence says coverage fails precisely on sparse/spiky units and degrades with horizon, so coverage must be audited by segment and over time rather than assumed.

### Cited Findings
- [ABS] Conformalized quantile regression combines any quantile regressor with split conformal calibration; it gives finite-sample marginal coverage without distributional assumptions, is "fully adaptive to heteroscedasticity" and tends to give shorter intervals than other conformal methods — [Romano, Patterson, Candès 2019, arXiv 1905.03222](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03222)
- [ABS] Conformal prediction works with any pre-trained model and has extensions for distribution shift, time series and outliers — [Angelopoulos & Bates 2021, arXiv 2107.07511](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07511)
- [ABS] Validity relies on exchangeability; under covariate shift a weighted version restores coverage when the likelihood ratio between test and training covariate distributions can be estimated — [Tibshirani et al. 2019, arXiv 1904.06019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06019)
- [ABS] "If the data distribution drifts over time, then the data points are no longer exchangeable"; weighted quantiles that up-weight recent observations give robustness with bounded coverage loss — [Barber et al. 2022, arXiv 2202.13415](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.13415)
- [ABS] For skewed data, separately calibrated one-sided lower and upper conformal bounds give tail-specific guarantees and better directional calibration than classical two-sided intervals (simulation plus a finance application) — [arXiv 2606.18199](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.18199)
- [FT] Field evidence of conformal failure on sparse units: in a production marketplace panel, split-conformal bands calibrated per buyer on earlier origins delivered 92% coverage at nominal 95% on smooth-demand buyers but "about 74%" on lumpy and intermittent buyers — "the bands are least trustworthy on precisely the customers whose forecasts are least trustworthy"; the same blind spot reproduced on M5 ("conformal bands under-cover most on the spikiest items") — [arXiv 2609.27867](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27867)
- [FT] Model-native intervals also under-cover at long horizons: 80% intervals of both classical methods and TimesFM-3 under-covered, TimesFM-3 more at longer horizons — [arXiv 2610.00589](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00589)
- [FT] The ZILN loss "provides a full prediction distribution": probability of returning plus a lognormal value distribution whose quantiles quantify uncertainty; the paper also notes Benoit & Van den Poel (2009) advocated quantile regression for CLV to obtain prediction intervals — [arXiv 1912.07753](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.07753)
- [ABS] NGBoost boosts the parameters of a conditional distribution using natural gradients, with any base learner, distribution family and scoring rule — [Duan et al. 2019, arXiv 1910.03225](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.03225)
- [ABS] Distributional gradient boosting on XGBoost and LightGBM backbones (the XGBoostLSS / LightGBMLSS line) models all parameters of a parametric distribution or approximates the conditional CDF with normalizing flows — [März & Kneib 2022, arXiv 2204.00778](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.00778)
- [FT] Caution on distributional GBDT at scale: probabilistic LightGBM heads were among the most computationally heavy models, unstable across seeds, and the Tweedie head failed numerically on M5 in one benchmark — [arXiv 2601.14031](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14031)
- [ABS] Deep ensembles: train several networks independently and combine; "simple to implement, readily parallelizable", well calibrated and more uncertain out of distribution — [Lakshminarayanan et al. 2016, arXiv 1612.01474](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474)
- [FT] TabICLv2 outputs quantiles and needs isotonic (PAVA) post-processing to keep them monotone — an example that multi-quantile outputs need a crossing fix — [arXiv 2602.11139](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11139)

### Inferences
- Scoring cost ranking (per user, relative to one LightGBM point model): split-conformal/CQR correction adds essentially nothing (one precomputed offset per calibration group); parametric distribution heads (ZILN, or mean plus dispersion) add one or two extra outputs; separate quantile GBDTs add one model per quantile; deep ensembles multiply neural inference by the number of members (typically 5), which directly eats the 30-minute budget; NGBoost's reference implementation is not built for 10⁸–10⁹ training rows (no evidence found of use at that size).
- Exchangeability analysis for this panel: users within one scoring week are plausibly exchangeable with a random held-out set of users from the same origin week, but the calibration labels need 52 matured weeks, so the calibration set is at least a year older than the scoring population. That is temporal shift; the remedies in the cited literature are covariate-shift weighting and recency weighting, and neither gives an exact guarantee. Expect to monitor realised coverage on partial-horizon labels (e.g. 13- and 26-week) as an early warning.
- For zero-inflated targets, a two-sided 90% interval for an individual user is often [0, upper] and uninformative at the bottom; one-sided upper bounds, or reporting P(>0) plus a conditional-positive interval, communicates more. The tail-specific conformal result supports calibrating the two tails separately.
- Group-wise (Mondrian) calibration by journey state × spend tier is the natural defence against the "under-cover on spiky units" failure: calibrate separately where residual distributions differ most.
- Individual intervals do not aggregate. Intervals for segment or cohort totals need either a joint model (simulation, section 5) or a block bootstrap over users and weeks; conformal prediction does not provide them.
- "Calibrated outputs" in the brief covers two different things: calibrated probabilities/means (fix with isotonic or decile-level recalibration on a recent matured cohort) and calibrated intervals (fix with CQR). Both are post-processing steps that are model-agnostic and therefore should be applied identically to every bake-off candidate so the comparison is of models, not of calibration effort.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that applies conformal prediction specifically to customer lifetime value (an arXiv search for the combination returned nothing).
- Quantile regression forests were not researched; no evidence on their cost at this scale.
- No published measurements of LightGBMLSS/XGBoostLSS or NGBoost training on 10⁸+ rows were found.
- No source was found comparing CQR against parametric ZILN intervals on LTV data; the recommendation rests on general properties.

## 7. Lift and causal use of LTV forecasts: pre-treatment forecast as covariate / control variate (CUPED, CUPAC), surrogate indices, uplift modeling on LTV. What properties must the forecast have for forecast revisions to be a valid lift measure?

### Takeaway
There are two distinct uses with very different requirements. A pre-treatment forecast used as a regression covariate (CUPED/CUPAC/MLRATE) only has to be fixed before assignment and correlated with the outcome — it cannot bias the estimate and needs no calibration. A post-treatment forecast or forecast revision used as the outcome is a surrogate index: it is unbiased for the long-term effect only if the features the model reads fully mediate the treatment's effect and the feature-to-outcome mapping is the same under treatment, and the recent evidence is that it works well on average but fails silently when those conditions break.

### Cited Findings
**Forecast as a pre-treatment covariate (variance reduction)**
- [SNIP] CUPED (Deng, Xu, Kohavi, Walker, WSDM 2013): adjust the outcome as Y − θ(X − X̄) with θ = Cov(Y,X)/Var(X); the covariate must be unaffected by the treatment, and variance reduction is tied to the squared correlation (a covariate correlated 0.7 with the outcome removes 49% of variance) — [ExP platform page](https://exp-platform.com/cuped/); [MetricGate explainer](https://metricgate.com/blogs/cuped-variance-reduction-ab-testing/)
- [SNIP] CUPAC (DoorDash, 2020) uses the output of an ML model trained on pre-experiment data as the control covariate; DoorDash reported "a nearly 40% reduction in required test length vs. baseline"; Etsy reported an average 27% variance reduction, "nearly 4x as much as CUPED", ranging from 2% to 77% across metrics — [DoorDash](https://careersatdoordash.com/?p=1650); [Etsy Code as Craft](https://www.etsy.com/be/codeascraft/reducing-experiment-duration-with-predicted-control-variates)
- [ABS] MLRATE (Meta): ML-predicted outcomes as a regression-adjustment covariate with cross-fitting; "robust to poor predictions": if predictions are uncorrelated with outcomes it is asymptotically no worse than difference-in-means; in A/A tests on 48 Facebook metrics it had over 70% lower variance than difference-in-means and about 19% lower than adjusting only for the pre-experiment value of the outcome — [Guo et al. 2021, arXiv 2106.07263](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.07263)
- [ABS] "In-experiment data are often more strongly correlated with the outcome, but using arbitrary post-treatment variables can introduce bias"; a framework combining pre- and in-experiment data gave substantial extra variance reduction at Etsy using only a few selected post-treatment covariates — [Lin & Crespo 2024, arXiv 2410.09027](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.09027)

**Forecast as the outcome (surrogate index)**
- [ABS] Surrogate index theory: with a two-sample design (experimental sample with treatment and surrogates; observational sample with surrogates and the long-term outcome), the long-term average treatment effect is identified if the proxies collectively satisfy surrogacy (treatment and outcome independent given the surrogates); the paper derives the bias from violations and notes "the validity of the surrogacy condition is often controversial" — [Athey, Chetty, Imbens, Kang, arXiv 1603.09326](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09326)
- [SNIP] The surrogate index is the predicted value of the long-term outcome given the short-term outcomes; in the job-training application a 9-year employment effect could have been estimated within 1.5 years with a 35% reduction in standard errors; published in Review of Economic Studies (2025) — [NBER w26463](https://www.nber.org/papers/26463)
- [FT] Netflix evaluation on 1,098 arms from 200 A/B tests: linear "auto-surrogate" indices built from 14 days of the outcome itself agreed with the 63-day direct read about 95% of the time; among launch decisions they achieved 79% precision and 65% recall; the mean difference between surrogate and direct estimates was "basically 0" but with fatter-than-normal tails; surrogate estimates had lower variance; no case of launching something measured as significantly negative. Models trained on the same users' pre-test data and on a similar past test gave closely aligned results — [Zhang et al. 2023, arXiv 2311.11922](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.11922)
- [ABS] LinkedIn: "because the surrogate rarely predicts the true north perfectly, a regular A/B test based on surrogate metrics tends to have high false positive rate"; the comparison must be adjusted for prediction uncertainty (case study: predicted confirmed hire) — [Duan, Ba, Zhang 2021, arXiv 2106.01421](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.01421)
- [SNIP] A write-up of that approach reports an R² of 0.69 for the surrogate model and that correcting the variance cut the number of significant experiments from 30 to 2 out of 203 — [Towards Data Science](https://towardsdatascience.com/how-to-reduce-a-b-testing-duration-using-surrogate-metrics-3631c6295039)
- [ABS] 2026 finite-sample study (seven estimators, eleven data-generating processes): "the surrogate index delivers large efficiency gains when surrogacy holds but its coverage collapses under violations", and there is "a band of violations [that] destroys surrogate-index coverage yet is too small to detect on most datasets"; prediction-powered inference variants (PPI++) stay asymptotically valid when a random subset has matured labels. Recommendation: PPI++ as primary analysis, "treating the surrogate index as a sensitivity analysis" — [arXiv 2609.30528](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30528)
- [ABS] Prediction-powered inference gives valid confidence intervals when a small labelled sample is supplemented with ML predictions on a large sample, "without making any assumptions on the machine-learning algorithm", and better predictions give tighter intervals; PPI++ adapts to prediction quality so intervals never get worse than labelled-only ones — [Angelopoulos et al. 2023, arXiv 2301.09633](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09633); [arXiv 2311.01453](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.01453)
- [ABS] Without assuming surrogacy, surrogates still improve precision when the long-term outcome is observed for a subset of units — [Kallus & Mao 2020, arXiv 2003.12408](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.12408)
- [ABS] The optimal proxy metric "is not apriori fixed; rather it should depend on the sample size (or effective noise level)" of the experiment; proxies can be built and validated from a corpus of past experiments — [Tripuraneni et al. 2023, arXiv 2309.07893](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07893)
- [ABS] Early precedent for exactly this use: detecting lifetime differences between groups early via "bootstrap hypothesis testing of the lifetime forecast of the response", applied in online advertising — [arXiv 1906.05959](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.05959)
- [SNIP] Industry practice: Dropbox multiplies predicted probability to spend by predicted two-year amount and calibrates "as a function of the trial day, the plan type, and the geographical region" before using it as an experiment metric; Wayfair averages predicted delayed rewards by arm, calls the identification assumption "fairly demanding", and recommends backtesting on historical tests at 15/30/60 days — [Dropbox Tech](https://dropbox.tech/machine-learning/accelerating-our-a-b-experiments-with-machine-learning-xr); [Wayfair Tech Blog](https://aboutwayfair.com/careers/tech-blog/speeding-up-a-b-tests-with-delayed-reward-forecasting)
- [SNIP] Warning case: across 190 Zillow tests, initial gains on a short-run metric "tend to coincide with downstream losses (e.g., six-month retention)" — [Marketing Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/mksc.2025.0164)

**Time-consistency of forecast revisions**
- [FT] "Ideal forecasts of a future event behave as martingales": for a fixed target Y_T, the sequence of forecasts made at t = 1…T is a martingale, so successive revisions are uncorrelated and mean-zero given the past. "Most processes are not martingales – you have to make them": the property applies to forecasts of a fixed target, not to rolling forecasts whose target moves. Diagnostics: calibration (E[p_t | p_s] = p_s for s < t, checked by plotting later against earlier forecasts) and total quadratic variation, which should equal π(1−π) for a threshold event; excess variation means "the forecast distribution is changing more than it should". A post-hoc "martingale filter" can repair volatility without refitting the model — [Foster & Stine 2021, arXiv 2105.06834](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.06834)

**Uplift modeling on LTV**
- [ABS] Revenue uplift models struggle with the "continuous long-tail response distribution"; RERUM plugs the ZILN loss into existing uplift architectures and adds uplift-ranking losses; validated offline and in large-scale online experiments at Tencent FiT — [arXiv 2405.15301](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15301)
- [SNIP] A 2026 preprint argues MSE-based meta-learners collapse toward near-zero uplift under >80% zeros with a few whales, and evaluates by area under the uplift curve — [VALOR, arXiv 2604.02472](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.02472v1)

### Inferences
Requirements for forecast revisions (ΔF = F after − F before) to be a valid lift measure, assembled from the cited theory:
- **Fixed target.** A rolling "next 52 weeks" forecast changes its target window every week, so its week-over-week change mixes the window shift with news. Revisions must be computed for a fixed window (e.g. value over calendar weeks t₀ to t₀+52, expressed as realised-to-date plus forecast-of-remainder). Only then does the martingale property apply. A model with direct fixed-horizon heads cannot produce this without retraining per remaining horizon; an HMM, a hazard model, or any per-week forecaster can.
- **Mean-zero revisions absent treatment (martingale / time-consistency).** If average revisions are systematically non-zero around predictable events — a season launch the calendar already announced, a holiday — the model was not pricing in known information and that jump will be misread as lift. Test: in untreated populations and placebo weeks, mean ΔF by segment should be zero and revisions should be serially uncorrelated; total revision variance should match the Foster–Stine benchmark.
- **Calibration in the mean at the level of the comparison.** Lift is a difference of means of forecasts, so what matters is that E[Y | F] = F within the treated and control populations (arm-level and segment-level mean calibration), not ranking quality. Decile calibration on a recent matured cohort is the minimum; calibration should be re-checked on users who have just experienced an event of the type being measured.
- **Surrogacy and comparability.** After treatment the forecast only sees the treatment through its input features (recent activity, hours, spend). The revision equals the long-term effect only if those features carry the whole effect and the historical mapping from features to 52-week outcomes holds for treated users. Treatments that pull forward activity (a burst of play that would have happened anyway), change the type of engagement, or shift spend timing violate this; the forecast will extrapolate the burst as a persistent state change. The Zillow and LinkedIn results show this is a practical, not theoretical, risk.
- **No leakage.** For the covariate use, the forecast must be frozen strictly before assignment/exposure, from a model whose training data does not include the experiment's outcomes (MLRATE's cross-fitting). For the outcome use, the model must not take treatment assignment or treatment-defined features as inputs, and must be the same model version before and after (a retrain between the two forecasts creates revisions that are model drift).
- **Use a control group; the revision alone is not the estimate.** The defensible estimator is difference-in-revisions between exposed and comparable unexposed users (the control's mean revision absorbs model drift and common shocks), ideally under randomisation. For non-randomised events such as a season launch everyone experiences, the revision measures "news relative to the model's expectation", which is an event-study quantity and not causal lift unless the model's pre-event expectation is trusted.
- **Correct inference.** Standard errors must include forecast error (Duan et al.), not just sampling noise in ΔF; otherwise false positives inflate. Where matured labels exist for a subset, PPI++ or the Kallus–Mao estimator gives valid intervals without the surrogacy assumption and should be the primary read, with the pure forecast-revision estimate as the fast, assumption-laden read.
- **Stability.** Models whose forecasts are jumpy from week to week (high revision variance not explained by news) make poor lift instruments; the TSFM-versus-Croston result that forecast instability reverses the ranking once plan changes have a cost is the forecasting analogue. Revision volatility should be a bake-off metric.
- Consequence for the bake-off: the pre-treatment forecast as a CUPAC covariate is essentially free and works with any candidate (gain grows with the forecast's R²). The forecast-revision-as-outcome use adds model-selection criteria that plain accuracy does not capture — fixed-target capability, revision mean-zero tests, revision volatility — and these favour models with explicit weekly dynamics (HMM, hazard, or sequence models with per-week heads) over a single 52-week-total regressor.

## 8. Evaluation methodology for long-horizon user-level forecasts: temporal versus user splits, overlapping label windows, calibration of probabilities and of means for heavy tails, proper scoring rules, paired bootstrap for model comparison

### Takeaway
The evaluation design can decide the winner more than the models do: recent evidence shows rankings flip with the unit of analysis, with point versus interval scoring, and with how much error is pooled in significance tests. For this bake-off that means fixing, before any model is fitted, an out-of-time split with full 52-week label separation, mean-eliciting scores for the expected-value targets, calibration by decile and segment, and user-clustered paired resampling for model differences.

### Cited Findings
- [FT] "A forecasting benchmark reports which method won. We show that the answer is set by the evaluator's choices before any model is fitted": moving the unit of analysis from market total to individual customer moved a production baseline from 2nd of 19 to 23rd of 25; on M5 the same baseline shape "places first at the market total and last per series" — [arXiv 2609.27867](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27867)
- [FT] Same paper on significance testing: a Diebold–Mariano test pooling 126 errors at the market total "finds no method significantly better", while pooling tens of thousands of per-buyer errors "finds almost everything significant at p<0.0001"; "pooled errors are also not independent. Buyers share calendar months, and one demand shock touches thousands of blocks at once", and the usual small-sample correction handles dependence over time, not across units. Their robustness check averaged error differences across units within each calendar period and re-ran the test on period-level averages — [arXiv 2609.27867](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27867)
- [FT] Same paper: point-accuracy and interval-quality rankings correlate 0.39 overall and 0.02 on intermittent series; "the best method on point accuracy ranks eleventh on interval quality" — [arXiv 2609.27867](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27867)
- [FT] Metric choice on zero-heavy series: MASE "is minimised by the conditional median, which is zero or near it", so an all-zero forecast scores best; sMAPE "is not informative"; mean-based RMSSE, bias, and pinball loss over deciles (a proper scoring rule, per Gneiting & Raftery 2007) tell a different story — [arXiv 2610.00589](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00589)
- [FT] For LTV specifically, Wang, Liu and Miao argue MSE "amplifies large prediction errors" and Pearson correlation is outlier-sensitive; they recommend evaluating discrimination with the normalized Gini coefficient (model Gini divided by label Gini; rank-based and "not sensitive to model miscalibration") and calibration with decile charts plus decile-level MAPE ("a well-calibrated model should have the prediction mean closely match to the label mean for each prediction decile"); Spearman correlation is used as a robust alternative — [arXiv 1912.07753](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.07753)
- [FT] Scaled quantile loss (quantile loss divided by that of the in-sample quantile) lets probabilistic scores be averaged across series of different scale; variability across random seeds was large enough for some model classes that a single training run "would be a single random draw" — [arXiv 2601.14031](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14031)
- [FT] Leakage-safe temporal evaluation in a user-churn/user-LTV benchmark: every training row carries a seed time, only information before the seed time may be used, data after the test timestamp is hidden at inference; the same entity can legitimately appear at multiple seed times ("this is not data leakage, since entity predictions are timestamp dependent") — [RelBench, arXiv 2407.20060](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.20060)
- [FT] Out-of-time and grouped splits are materially harder than i.i.d. splits: tabular foundation models that lead i.i.d. leaderboards "fall behind tuned conventional models on grouped or temporal splits" — [arXiv 2609.17895](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17895)
- [SNIP] Overlapping label windows: when labels are built over overlapping time windows, standard k-fold leaks; purged cross-validation removes training observations whose label window overlaps the test set and adds an embargo buffer, with a practitioner rule that the embargo be at least the prediction horizon (López de Prado, 2017/2018) — [Wikipedia: Purged cross-validation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purged_cross-validation); [temporalcv docs](https://temporalcv.readthedocs.io/en/latest/api/cv_financial.html)
- [SNIP] Model-comparison tests under overlapping horizons: extensions of Diebold–Mariano incorporate cross-covariances from overlapping forecast windows; DM power falls as dependence rises and it can spuriously reject; multi-horizon superior-predictive-ability tests use moving block bootstraps and an earlier version had "massive size distortions" — [CQE working paper 106/2023](https://www.wiwi.uni-muenster.de/cqe/sites/cqe/files/CQE_Paper/cqe_wp_106_2023.pdf)
- [FT] Survival-style evaluation practice: integrated Brier score with inverse-probability-of-censoring weights, time-dependent concordance, horizon-specific calibration gaps, and no-refit bootstrap resampling to qualify rankings as "directional signals, not claims of strict superiority"; results reported within model family because survival metrics are sensitive to the temporal representation — [arXiv 2604.08870](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.08870)
- [FT] Task definition matters as much as the metric: methods that are best at next-step prediction are often not best over the horizon, and a metric that looks reasonable (OTD) can rank a trivial baseline first — [HoTPP, arXiv 2406.14341](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.14341)
- [FT] Field experiments have fat-tailed effect distributions; differences between surrogate and direct reads at Netflix were "fatter-tailed compared to a normal distribution" — a reason to prefer resampling over normal approximations when comparing forecast-based reads — [arXiv 2311.11922](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.11922)

### Inferences
- **Split design.** The primary test must be out-of-time: features up to origin week T, labels over T+1…T+52, with all training labels ending no later than T (a full 52-week purge between the last training origin and the test origin). Because season position matters, use several test origins at different calendar phases (immediately before a launch, mid-season, off-season). A held-out-user split is a secondary check on generalisation to unseen users/new cohorts; alone it is optimistic because it shares calendar shocks between train and test.
- **Overlapping windows.** Weekly origins with 52-week labels overlap by 51 weeks, so (i) rows from adjacent origins are not independent training examples — more weekly snapshots add less information than their count suggests; (ii) any validation fold must be purged by 52 weeks from training origins; (iii) significance tests that treat user-origin rows as independent overstate confidence. Evaluate on a small number of well-separated origins rather than every week.
- **Scores per target.** P(any active week) and P(spend > 0): log loss and Brier score plus reliability curves by segment. Expected active weeks, hours, spend: these are means, so use mean-eliciting scores — Poisson deviance for active weeks, Tweedie or gamma deviance (or MSE on a winsorised scale as a sensitivity check) for hours and spend — plus normalized Gini for ranking and decile calibration with decile-level MAPE. Do not use MAE, MASE or MAPE as headline metrics for the mean targets: on zero-inflated data they reward predicting zero. For full distributions: pinball loss over a quantile grid / CRPS, reported separately from point metrics because rankings can be unrelated.
- **Calibration of means with heavy tails.** Decile charts hide the top: add a top-1% and top-0.1% bucket, and report aggregate bias (sum of predictions over sum of actuals) by country, platform, tenure and journey state, since lift measurement (section 7) depends on segment-level mean calibration.
- **Model comparison.** Use a paired bootstrap that resamples users (the cluster), keeping each user's predictions from all models and all origins together, and report the distribution of the score difference; repeat across test origins and treat origin-to-origin variation as the more honest uncertainty, because one calendar shock affects all users in an origin at once. Report seed-to-seed variance for neural candidates. Apply the same post-hoc calibration procedure to every candidate before scoring.
- **Unit of analysis.** Report both per-user metrics and aggregate metrics (total hours/spend by cohort and by segment). A model can win one and lose the other; the business uses (individual targeting versus cohort valuation versus lift) map to different levels.
- **Additional bake-off metrics implied by sections 5–7:** interval coverage by segment and horizon; revision volatility and placebo-week mean revision; scoring wall-clock for 100M users on the target hardware.
