# LTV bake-off needs no new candidates

Your guess holds on the main question, (a): **no new candidate model family needs to join the bake-off.** The four papers you supplied, and the works they build on, vary two things: the encoder (engineered features with boosted trees, or a sequence model) and the output head (ZILN and its successors). Candidates A and C already span both, and B covers the latent-state models of the marketing literature. The plan's gaps sit below the level of candidates. Among (b) reference baselines, one is missing: a discrete-time buy-till-you-die model, the classical benchmark this literature positions against, which costs almost nothing to score and is expected to lose. Among (c) techniques inside existing candidates, six are worth adopting, the most important being hours and spend outputs at more than one horizon and at least one alternative to the ZILN head. The evaluation plan needs additions to its scorecard and its resampling, not a redesign. Three caveats limit how hard to lean on this. The researchers could not open Bauer and Jannach, so every statement about it rests on search snippets and on how OptDist describes it. Nearly all the industrial evidence concerns new users over 1 to 30 days: no paper found tests a rolling 52-week forecast on a weekly panel of existing users, and none reports scoring time for a full user base. Several recommendations are the researchers' inference, not a paper's finding, and are marked as such.

## The four papers vary encoders and heads the plan already spans

Two of the four are output-head papers for a single scalar target, and two are sequence-model papers. None proposes a family outside feature-based trees, sequence networks, and the parametric models they benchmark against.

| Paper | What it proposes | Setting | Headline result | Relation to the plan |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bauer and Jannach 2021 (not opened; snippets only) | Encoder-decoder RNN with temporal convolutions, stacked with a gradient-boosting machine | E-commerce purchases; horizon unknown | Sequence model "competitive" alone, stack reported better; no numbers recovered | A and C cover the parts; the stack is not planned |
| Ma et al., arXiv 2412.20211 | Autoregressive token decoder in place of a regression head; a watch-time paper with an LTV side experiment | Two public LTV sets (30 days, one year) | MAE 12.996 against 15.784 for OptDist on Criteo-SSC; no Gini or calibration reported | A new head; not recommended |
| Chen et al., arXiv 2412.20295 | Dilated RNN on user-level weekly series, with zero-week subsampling | Existing users, Meta Quest revenue, 4 to 26 weeks | Adjusted SMAPE 22.9% / 39.8% / 51.2% against LightGBM 30.4% / 53.7% / 61.1% and BTYD 53.6% / 70.5% / 78.3% | C covers it; the sparse-token variant matches the subsampling |
| Weng et al., arXiv 2408.08585 (OptDist) | Several ZILN heads with a learned per-user selector | New users and clicks, 30 days and one year | Normalized Gini +1.6% to +5.5% and MAE −2.2% to −24.4% against one ZILN head (researchers' arithmetic) | A head ablation inside C |

Chen et al. is the only one of the four with data shaped like yours: sparse weekly series per user, existing users, rolling horizons. It favours the sequence model without settling A against C. The dilated RNN's error reduction relative to LightGBM is about 25% at 4 and 13 weeks and **16% at 26 weeks, half your horizon** (the researchers' arithmetic on the paper's adjusted-SMAPE table). The paper gives no user counts, loss function or variance. On its Uber new-user task a plain RNN lost to XGBoost (RMSE 2,830 against 2,292) and edged ahead only once city embeddings were added (2,271) ([Chen et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.20295)). Trees are often competitive when papers include them, and many head papers include none: XGBoost had the lowest MAE of any model in Huawei's comparison ([Wu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.14400)). A is a serious contender, not a formality.

The two head papers are measured on benchmarks unlike your problem. Criteo-SSC is click-level 30-day revenue, and the Kaggle set is 90% positive with features from a first purchase. On Kaggle, the only one-year horizon among the public sets, every head lands between **0.6693 (ZILN) and 0.6814 (OptDist) normalized Gini**, so head choice moved one-year results by a few percent ([Weng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.08585)). The generative-regression paper reports neither Gini nor any calibration metric for LTV, its inference cost scales with vocabulary size times decoded length, and its authors measured 10.2% lower serving throughput than the production control ([Ma et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.20211)). The researchers add that its decoded value is a greedy path and not a distributional mean, so calibrated expectations are untested.

The lineage behind the four is consistent. ZILN is the shared reference point, a baseline in three later papers and the source of the normalized-Gini and decile-chart convention ([Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.07753)). The buy-till-you-die (BTYD) family is the classical baseline, two-stage classify-then-regress models are the usual simple comparison, and Kuaishou's ODMN is the industrial multi-horizon design ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.13358)). OptDist calls sequence encoders "perpendicular" to distribution modelling and combinable with it, which is the two-axis view used above ([Weng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.08585)). On the researchers' mapping, R and A sit at the feature end of the encoder axis, C at the sequence end, and B is closest in spirit to BTYD. On the head axis the plan has only ZILN and sigmoids. The gaps are therefore a classical baseline and head variety, not a family.

Bauer and Jannach remains the open item. The DOI, the ACM page and the author-hosted PDF were all unreachable ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3441444)). Unverified: its horizon, metrics, margins, how the stack is wired, and whether its decoder emits per-period outputs that would bear on C's weekly heads.

## Other families fold into A, B or C, or break the scoring budget

Scoring 100M users in 30 minutes means about 56,000 users per second, and that figure eliminates most adjacent families before accuracy enters.

**Survival and hazard models are A or B under another name.** On a weekly panel the natural survival model is a discrete-time hazard on person-week rows, which is a classifier on stacked data and approximately equivalent to Cox regression when the classifier is logistic ([Craig et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.13480)). A 2026 benchmark of weekly dropout found all five weekly person-period models, linear to boosted to neural, inside a 0.0011 band of integrated Brier score ([arXiv 2604.08870](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.08870)). Trained in LightGBM this is a variant of A; with a return hazard added it is the observed-state special case of B. The researchers found no benchmark of hazard against direct 52-week prediction for LTV. Their structural argument is that the framing buys fresher training data (cohorts whose 52-week label has not matured still contribute) and per-week forecasts. It is an optional variant of A, and the cheapest way to give A week-level forecasts if item 2 is adopted. Single-event survival models assume churn is permanent, the opposite of season-driven returns.

**Neural point processes and trajectory simulation lose on both evidence and cost.** In the one benchmark built for long-horizon event forecasting, simple statistical baselines won or tied in 6 of 10 tasks, and evaluation can take several hours on one GPU for datasets of up to tens of millions of events ([Karpukhin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.14341)). Your panel is already weekly, which removes the continuous timestamps that are these models' structural advantage. Sampling 100 paths of 52 weekly steps for 100M users needs about 290 million network steps per second to finish in 30 minutes (the researchers' arithmetic). B's analytic forward recursion gives the same coherent weekly distribution without sampling.

**Foundation models reproduce the persistence baseline at far higher cost.** On a simulated series with about 70% zeros, TimesFM-3 "behaves much like the empirical distribution of the history", and so did the other foundation models tested ([arXiv 2610.00589](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00589)). Chronos-2's published 300 series per second implies roughly 185 GPUs to score 100M users in 30 minutes for one target, by the researchers' arithmetic ([Ansari et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15821)). Tabular foundation models recommend at most 1M context rows, and their own technical report concedes they trail tuned conventional models on temporal splits and large tables ([arXiv 2609.17895](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17895)). A zero-shot model on a user sample is an optional extra baseline; R's persistence baseline already occupies that slot.

**Graph, LLM and cross-domain methods solve a problem you do not have.** They report large gains, but for new users with little history: removing the graph embeddings raised error from 0.188 to 0.208 in a WeChat mini-games model built on shared user and game interactions ([Chen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11037)). With years of each user's own history and no social graph in the stated data, the researchers judge their expected value low and their scoring cost high.

**Neural versions of BTYD are worth tracking, not adding.** Two 2026 preprints fit BTYD likelihoods with amortized variational inference. The largest demonstrated scale is 5 million customers fitted in 8 minutes, both are continuous-time purchase models, and on the researchers' reading neither supports time-varying covariates such as a season calendar ([Ghosh and Schein](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19022); [Näf et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22636)).

**B has no competitor in the literature and no validation either.** The marketing papers that allow lapse and return through hidden Markov models report individual-level gains over BTYD, with BTYD often best on aggregates ([Romero et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/joinma/v27y2013i3p185-208.html)). That finding is snippet-only, the panels were small, and the researchers found no comparison of an HMM with boosted trees or sequence networks at any scale. Nothing else examined supplies interpretable journey states, so B stays, and the bake-off is its first real test.

## One baseline and six techniques earn a place

### Discrete-time BTYD is the benchmark outsiders will expect (item 1)

BTYD is the one standard approach named across the seed papers' lineage that R, A, B and C do not include. For weekly active-or-not data the right member is BG/BB, described as the discrete-time analogue of Pareto/NBD, with a death opportunity each period instead of each transaction (snippet-level; the original paper was not opened) ([Hardie](https://brucehardie.com/papers/020)). The better-known BG/NBD ties death to transactions and so assumes heavy users churn faster ([Ghosh and Schein](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19022)). BG/BB's forecasts are closed-form in log-gamma functions, which the researchers verified in the package source ([Lifetimes](https://pypi.org/project/Lifetimes/)).

Expect it to lose, with the caveat that no head-to-head found uses BG/BB itself or an engagement target such as active weeks. An unspecified BTYD variant was last at every horizon at Meta Quest by a wide margin ([Chen et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.20295)). On a free-to-play game, the error of two continuous-time BTYD models on the top 20% of spenders was about double that of deep models, on only 2,505 paying users ([Chen et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12799)). BG/BB has no mechanism for season-driven returns, and no package the researchers inspected offers BG/BB with covariates. The case for adding it is credibility and diagnosis. It is a label-free structural forecast against which leakage in A and C would show. It is also B with two states, an absorbing "dead" state and no covariates, so the gap between B and BG/BB measures what return-capable states and the season calendar buy; that nesting is the researchers' inference, consistent with a marketing paper that treats BG/BB as a constrained HMM ([Schwartz et al.](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:33:y:2014:i:2:p:188-205), snippet-only).

Two cautions apply. The cost claim is the researchers' own measurement, not a published one: scoring is a table lookup on three integers, the full table for histories up to 260 weeks took 171 seconds on one core, and 10 million lookups took 0.38 seconds, all on simulated data. Your histories run past 300 weeks, so the table would be larger than the one timed. And the baseline must be scored on its 52-week return probability and expected active weeks, not on P(alive): in one seven-year study of a related model (MBG/NBD), summed P(alive) overshot realised 18-month returners by 2.25 times while the same model's horizon forecast was off by 1.18 times ([Ulrich](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18623)).

### ZILN ranks well but misses the level (item 3)

The evidence that a plain ZILN head is beatable is consistent, and it concentrates where your targets are most exposed. Expected hours and expected spend are level quantities, and ZILN's weak points are aggregate bias and the top end. At Kuaishou, ZILN's absolute mean bias on an active-days label grew from **0.13 at 30 days to 8.66 at 365 days**, against 0.04 to 0.31 for the multi-horizon alternative ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.13358)). At Meituan, on chronological splits of 33M to 248M records, ZILN's bias was 12.81, 5.73 and 70.66 across three domains against 4.85, 0.90 and 21.18 for an ordinal-bucket model ([Zhao et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.10176)). In a Tencent game, ZILN matched a log-target MLP on Gini (0.9581 against 0.9605) but had top-5% MAPE of 0.75 against 0.48 ([Cao et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15944)). No source tests ZILN on hours.

No alternative wins consistently when another group re-implements it. Kuaishou's bucketed head fell to 0.4383 normalized Gini against ZILN's 0.6338 in OptDist's re-run ([Weng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.08585)). The practical reading is to keep ZILN as the default and run two alternatives in C: a mixture head (several ZILN heads with a selector, or a zero-inflated mixture of lognormals) and an ordinal-bucket head with an in-bucket regressor. The researchers note that OptDist selects one component per user at inference, so it does not enrich the interval; a true mixture does. For A, the intermittent-demand literature found the Tweedie family gave the best upper quantiles, while probabilistic LightGBM heads were unstable across training runs ([Damato et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14031)). That supports LightGBM's built-in Tweedie mean objective as an ablation beside ZILN, and reporting seed variance for A's ZILN variant. Your plan's aggregate-bias and decile-calibration checks will expose the problem if it occurs; the ablations give you something to switch to.

### Hours and spend need more than one horizon (item 2)

As the plan was described to the researchers, activity has 52 weekly outputs while hours and spend each have one head on the 52-week total. That has two costs. The first is accuracy. Kuaishou's ablation shows that coupling 30, 90, 180 and 365-day outputs lowered 365-day bias from 0.65 to 0.31 against a plain shared-bottom network, and that the longest horizon gained most ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.13358)). The label there is activity for new users, so transfer to heavy-tailed spend is unproven. The second cost is the researchers' inference and matters more. Your fixed-window martingale check needs, at each later week, a forecast of the remainder of a fixed window. A model with only a 52-week-total head cannot produce a 40-week or 13-week remainder; B can, and so can C for activity. Cumulative heads at 4, 13, 26 and 52 weeks, parameterized as non-negative increments so ordering holds by construction, give revisions at those spacings; weekly heads give week-over-week revisions. Shorter-horizon labels also mature sooner, which gives an early read on calibration drift. One paper found horizons can conflict as training tasks, so confirm the 52-week metrics do not degrade ([Chen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11037)).

### Inputs and tail handling matter more than the encoder (items 4 to 6)

Where encoders are compared directly, differences are small: on WeChat mini-games an LSTM, a Transformer variant and a temporal convolution network scored normalized Gini of 0.940, 0.944 and 0.940 ([Chen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11037)), and at Huawei swapping the backbone moved ZILN's error less than changing the head did ([Wu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.14400)). Inputs moved results more. In a freemium game, an LSTM combined with aggregate features reached churn AUC of about 0.873 against 0.859 for the LSTM alone ([Kristensen and Burelli](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.03184)), which supports feeding A's features into C at no scoring cost. The stack of A and C rests on the unread Bauer and Jannach paper; run it as a diagnostic of how much signal each model leaves, since a production stack means maintaining two models.

Top-end under-prediction is reported by the game papers that check, including an over-prediction of 344% in the lowest value band and under-prediction of 49.8% in the highest at WeChat ([Chen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11037)). Two remedies have evidence: a high-spender detector used as an auxiliary task and gate, which raised normalized Gini by 1.5% to 6.2% over the best baseline on three Tencent games ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12729)), and a top-bucket module that cut bias by 25% at Meituan ([Zhao et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.10176)). Monte Carlo dropout also helps the top end but multiplies scoring passes.

The season calendar is the least-evidenced item and the cheapest. Kuaishou, Douyin and the Meta paper all handle calendar effects by feeding calendar covariates, and Douyin feeds them over the forecast horizon, for channel-level curves ([Wan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.17639)). The researchers found **no peer-reviewed paper on season or battle-pass effects in LTV models**, which they call the largest gap relative to your product. Features such as weeks until the next launch and launches inside the window are their suggestion, not a tested result.

Four planned choices have direct support and need no change. Weekly sigmoids for activity avoid the long-horizon bias ZILN showed on Kuaishou's bounded activity count, on the researchers' reading. Running both GRU and Transformer is right because nothing shows the Transformer winning. The sparse-token variant corresponds to Chen et al.'s zero-week subsampling, which adds "weeks since last step" and week-of-year features to compensate for uneven steps. Keeping masked pretraining optional matches Revolut's finding that a pretrained foundation model changed LTV PR-AUC by only −1.2% to +1.8% against a task-specific baseline ([Ostroukhov et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.08649)).

## The evaluation plan holds; the lift use needs the most care

Your scorecard already exceeds the literature's. The researchers found no LTV paper that evaluates CRPS or interval coverage, and many headline results come from random splits, so expect smaller gains under your out-of-time split than the papers report. The changes are items 7 to 11 in the table.

**Tail and level of analysis (item 8).** Decile charts hide the top, so add top 1% and 0.1% buckets and bias by true-value band. OptDist and the Meituan paper also report rank metrics on positive-label users only, which separates ranking payers from detecting them ([Weng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.08585)). Score per user and on cohort totals, because rankings flip: in a production forecasting benchmark, moving the unit of analysis from market total to individual customer moved one baseline from 2nd of 19 to 23rd of 25, and point-accuracy and interval-quality rankings correlated only 0.02 on intermittent series ([arXiv 2609.27867](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27867)). Do not adopt Chen et al.'s adjusted SMAPE as a headline: SMAPE-type and median-eliciting metrics reward zero forecasts on zero-heavy data ([arXiv 2610.00589](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00589)).

**Resampling (item 9).** The same benchmark found that a test pooling tens of thousands of per-unit errors called almost everything significant at p < 0.0001, because units share calendar shocks. Resample users as clusters, evaluate at several well-separated origins in different season phases with a full 52-week purge, and treat origin-to-origin variation as the honest uncertainty. The purging rule comes from finance and is snippet-only.

**One calibration layer for every candidate (item 7).** In the one study with numbers, an isotonic recalibration fitted on one matured scoring date brought every model within 1.06 to 1.13 times actuals on the next, while uncorrected bias drifted from 1.00 to 1.18 over three scoring years ([Ulrich](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18623)). Conformalized quantile regression wraps any quantile model with a held-out correction ([Romano et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03222)), which the researchers note is a table lookup at scoring time. In field data, though, split-conformal bands covered about 74% at a nominal 95% on intermittent units ([arXiv 2609.27867](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27867)), and your calibration cohort is necessarily a year old. Applying the same layer to every candidate, calibrating by group, and auditing coverage by segment are the researchers' inference; no paper applies conformal prediction to LTV.

**Scorecard additions (item 10).** No paper reports throughput for a full user base. Kuaishou states it scores all users daily in batch but gives no timings ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.13358)), so the 30-minute constraint has to be measured on your hardware for each candidate. Revision volatility and interval coverage by segment belong on the scorecard for the same reason: they are stated wants that plain accuracy does not capture.

**Lift (item 11).** A forecast frozen before treatment and used as a regression covariate cannot bias an experiment and needs no calibration; at Meta this cut variance by over 70% against a difference in means ([Guo et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.07263)). A forecast revision used as the outcome is a surrogate index. Netflix found 14-day surrogates agreed with 63-day reads about 95% of the time ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.11922)), but LinkedIn reports high false-positive rates unless forecast error enters the comparison ([Duan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.01421)), and a 2026 study finds coverage collapses under violations too small to detect ([arXiv 2609.30528](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30528)). The martingale property your check relies on holds only for a fixed target ([Foster and Stine](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.06834)). The researchers' assembled requirements are a fixed window, one model version before and after, a control group whose mean revision absorbs drift, and a placebo test that mean revisions are zero around launches the calendar already announced. No paper analyses week-over-week LTV forecast changes as a lift estimator.

## Conclusion

The literature cannot pick your winner. Its nearest evidence stops at 26 weeks with a narrowing margin between trees and sequence models, it contains no test of an HMM against either, and it has nothing on seasons. Your bake-off will be one of the larger direct comparisons of these families, which shifts the weight from which models enter to how they are scored. The stated wants that separate the candidates are not accuracy metrics: fixed-window revisions, journey states and intervals all favour models with explicit weekly dynamics. Item 2 is therefore a fairness condition as much as an accuracy technique. Without it, A and C cannot produce the fixed-window forecasts for hours and spend that the revision check needs and that B supplies in closed form.

Two follow-ups would close the remaining gaps. Someone with ACM access should read Bauer and Jannach to confirm how its stack is wired and whether its decoder design bears on C's weekly heads. And journey states deserve a validation rule before B is fitted: in the buy-till-you-die family, specifications with nearly identical forecasts produced "alive" counts from 3,654 to 27,734 on one panel ([Ulrich](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18623)). Extending that warning to B's latent states is this report's inference, not a finding, but it argues for defining each state by its observable 52-week outcomes and not by its label.

## Addendum, 2026-11-18: Bauer and Jannach read in full

After this report was written, Alex supplied the PDF of Bauer and Jannach, so the paper has now been read in full (all sections through the conclusions). The reading confirms the report's answer and firms up two items. Reading notes are in `research_notes/Modern LTV modeling approaches/bauer_jannach_2021.md`.

- **Still no new candidate.** Its two models are trees on engineered features and a GRU sequence-to-sequence network, which are candidates A and C. On 1.08M retail customers over a 26-week horizon they tie: the sequence model has the lower RMSE (12.16 against 12.50) and the trees the lower MAE (3.42 against 3.91).
- **The stack (item 5) moves from weak to moderate evidence.** A second-level boosted model on both models' predictions cut RMSE by about 4% and MAE by about 8% against the best single model, significant on the large dataset. The plan keeps it as a diagnostic with that method.
- **Feeding aggregate features to the sequence model (item 5) is now the best-evidenced input change.** In the paper's ablation, removing the engineered features cost more than simplifying the architecture or dropping embeddings. The plan makes it the default for candidate C.
- **Output granularity did not matter.** Weekly, 13-week and single-total outputs gave RMSE within 0.15, which supports the multi-horizon heads of item 2 as accuracy-neutral.
- **The paper's limits for this decision:** profit is the only target, the horizon is 26 weeks, there are no seasons, wholesale outliers were removed, and it reports no ranking, calibration or scoring-time results.

The statements about this paper in the body of the report were written from search snippets; where they differ from this addendum, the addendum is correct.
