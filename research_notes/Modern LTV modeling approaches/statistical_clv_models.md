## 1. BTYD models: assumptions, sufficient statistics, closed-form outputs, covariates and seasonality, software, scoring cost

### Takeaway
The classic BTYD models (Pareto/NBD, BG/NBD, MBG/NBD, BG/BB) compress each user to three numbers (frequency, recency, observation length) and return P(alive) and expected future transactions in closed form, so fitting and scoring are cheap; the price is a Poisson or Bernoulli "buy while alive, die once, never return" story with no calendar time. Covariates and seasonality exist only in specific extensions (time-varying covariates only for Pareto/NBD in CLVTools, at a large fitting cost), and regularity-aware variants (Pareto/GGG) need MCMC unless replaced by the 2026 variational versions.

### Cited Findings

**Model structure and sufficient statistics**
- [FT] BTYD models split behaviour into a transaction process ("buys") and a lifetime process ("dies"). Pareto/NBD (Schmittlein, Morrison, Colombo 1987): Poisson transactions until a Pareto-distributed death time; heterogeneity in the purchase rate and the death rate is Gamma; four parameters. — [Ghosh & Schein 2026, arXiv 2608.19022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19022); [Chen et al. 2018, arXiv 1811.12799](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12799)
- [FT] Pareto/NBD and (M)BG/NBD "emit a sufficient statistic representation such that the data on each customer need only consist of their frequency, recency, and total time", which allows "implementations directly in Excel". — [arXiv 2608.19022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19022)
- [FT] BG/NBD (Fader, Hardie, Lee 2005) replaces the lifetime process by assuming death can only coincide with a transaction (Beta-mixed Geometric). MBG/NBD (Batislam et al. 2007) additionally allows death before any repeat transaction. Because death is tied to transactions, "(M)BG/NBD assumes high-volume customers churn faster (Jerath et al., 2011), an assumption not made by Pareto/NBD and one that is often violated in practice." — [arXiv 2608.19022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19022)
- [FT] Plain BG/NBD forces P(alive) = 1 for customers with no repeat purchase; in a one-time-buyer-heavy base this produced inverted ranking (AUC 0.32) when P(alive) was scored against 18-month repeat purchase; MBG/NBD and later variants repair this. — [Ulrich 2026, arXiv 2607.18623](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18623)
- [FT] Periodic death opportunity model (Jerath, Fader, Hardie 2011) "decouples death from purchases"; Pareto/GGG "generalizes purchase timing". — [arXiv 2607.18623](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18623)
- [SN] BG/BB (Fader, Hardie, Shang 2010, Marketing Science 29(6):1086-1108) is "the discrete-time analog of the Pareto/NBD": Beta-Bernoulli purchasing at each discrete opportunity while alive, Beta-Geometric permanent dropout; four parameters (alpha, beta for purchase; gamma, delta for dropout); data condense to a recency-frequency matrix; "relatively simple closed-form expressions for the expected number of future transactions conditional on past observed behavior"; implementable in a spreadsheet; demonstrated on nonprofit donation data. — [Bruce Hardie paper page](https://brucehardie.com/papers/020); [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:29:y:2010:i:6:p:1086-1108)
- [SN] In the R BTYD package, BG/BB P(alive) takes frequency x, recency t.x and number of calibration opportunities n.cal and returns the probability of being alive at the next transaction opportunity. — [rdrr.io BTYD bgbb.PAlive](https://rdrr.io/cran/BTYD/man/bgbb.PAlive.html)
- [FT] Pareto/GGG (Platzer & Reutterer 2016): Gamma renewal with customer-specific regularity k; keeps sufficiency with one extra statistic (sum of log inter-transaction times); marginal likelihood is intractable, so it is fitted by MCMC, which is "substantially more computationally intensive and less scalable"; covariates are not available in BTYDplus, the main open-source implementation. — [arXiv 2608.19022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19022)
- [FT] (M)BG/CNBD-k (Reutterer et al. 2021, "Pareto/GGG the easy way"): one global integer regularity k (Erlang-k), fitted by MLE, cannot express "clumpy" timing, and inherits the (M)BG/NBD link between death and transactions. — [arXiv 2608.19022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19022)
- [FT] Pareto/GGG was reported to outperform Pareto/NBD in out-of-sample individual-level forecasts when inter-transaction timing is even mildly regular, to perform on par when it is not, with the largest gains in the high-frequency, low-recency segment (as summarised by a secondary paper). — [Pollak 2021, arXiv 2102.05771](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.05771)

**Closed-form outputs (verified against package source)**
- [FT] BG/NBD conditional expected purchases over horizon t in Lifetimes 0.11.3 is a closed form using one Gauss hypergeometric function per customer, divided by `1 + (x>0)*(a/(b+x-1))*((alpha+T)/(alpha+recency))**(r+x)`; the denominator's reciprocal is the BG/NBD P(alive). — [Lifetimes on PyPI (source of 0.11.3 inspected)](https://pypi.org/project/Lifetimes/)
- [FT] BG/BB conditional expected purchases over the next m periods in Lifetimes is `(1/L) * B(alpha+x+1, beta+n-x)/B(alpha,beta) * delta/(gamma-1) * G(gamma+delta)/G(1+delta) * [G(1+delta+n)/G(gamma+delta+n) - G(1+delta+n+m)/G(gamma+delta+n+m)]`, where L is the individual likelihood, B the beta function and G the gamma function. Only log-beta and log-gamma evaluations are needed, no hypergeometric function. The Lifetimes BG/BB fitter accepts `weights`, i.e. it can be fitted on the recency-frequency table with counts. — [Lifetimes on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/Lifetimes/)
- [FT] Pareto/NBD estimation "requires multiple evaluations of the Gauss Hypergeometric Function", which BG/NBD mitigates. — [Burelli 2022, arXiv 2209.12619](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.12619)
- [FT] For all Pareto/NBD variants CLVTools provides conditional expected transactions (CET), P(alive), and discounted expected residual transactions (DERT, infinite horizon); with time-varying covariates DERT is replaced by DECT over a finite horizon. — [Meierer et al. 2026, arXiv 2602.09845](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09845)
- [FT] A fitted BTYD model gives three per-customer outputs that practice tends to conflate: aliveness A = P(alive | x, t_x, T); expected transactions in a window E[X_H]; and the horizon-return probability R_H = P(X_H >= 1), "the continuous-time counterpart of the conditional penetration that Fader, Hardie, and Shang (2010) recommend reporting alongside P(alive)". A >= R_H always. — [arXiv 2607.18623](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18623)

**Spend: Gamma-Gamma**
- [FT] Gamma-Gamma expected average transaction value in Lifetimes: a weighted average of the customer's observed mean and the population mean, with individual weight `p*x/(p*x+q-1)` and population mean `v*p/(q-1)`; inputs are frequency and mean monetary value. — [Lifetimes on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/Lifetimes/)
- [FT] The frequency-times-value decomposition "relies on a shaky assumption that purchase order value is independent of purchase frequency". — [Wang, Liu, Miao 2019 (ZILN), arXiv 1912.07753](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.07753)
- [FT] Multiplying DERT by expected spend assumes spending is stationary. — [arXiv 2602.09845](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09845)
- [FT] Glady et al. showed the independence assumption fails on several real data sets and proposed a dependent variant. — [arXiv 2209.12619](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.12619)

**Covariates and seasonality**
- [SN] Bachmann, Meierer, Näf (2021, Marketing Science 40(4):783-809) extend Pareto/NBD to time-invariant and time-varying contextual factors on the purchase process, the attrition process or both, keeping a closed-form maximum-likelihood solution; on three retail data sets, time-varying factors improved out-of-sample predictions of purchases and attrition. — [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:40:y:2021:i:4:p:783-809)
- [FT] Feature table in the CLVTools paper: time-invariant covariates are supported by BTYDplus and CLVTools, not by BTYD or Lifetimes; time-varying covariates, correlation between processes, regularisation and equality constraints only by CLVTools, and only for Pareto/NBD and its variants. CLVTools does not include BG/BB, Pareto/GGG or hierarchical-Bayes Pareto/NBD; it "focuses on continuous-time probabilistic models with closed-form marginal likelihoods". — [arXiv 2602.09845](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09845)
- [FT] CLVTools case study: a weekly `High.Season` indicator as a time-varying covariate fits seasonal spikes in the tracking plot much better than the no-covariate and static-covariate models. Prediction requires covariate values for the whole prediction period, supplied by the analyst. With time-varying covariates, inter-purchase times are no longer i.i.d. — [arXiv 2602.09845](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09845)
- [FT] CLVAE authors: adding time-varying covariates such as seasonality indicators to their VAE-based model "would require defining time-varying latent variables" and is left to future work. — [Näf, Mbelson, Meierer 2026, arXiv 2604.22636](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22636)
- [FT] Practitioner critique: "it is hard to incorporate useful features, such as seasonality, into a BTYD model, as the model itself is a pure vintage based model". — [Chen, Ng, Smyl, Steininger 2024, arXiv 2412.20295](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.20295)

**Software (current state)**
- [FT] PyMC-Marketing 1.4.0 (requires Python >= 3.12) ships `BetaGeoModel` (BG/NBD), `ModifiedBetaGeoModel`, `ParetoNBDModel`, `BetaGeoBetaBinomModel` (BG/BB, "non-contractual, discrete purchase opportunities"), `ShiftedBetaGeoModel` (contractual), `GammaGammaModel`. Covariate code exists in the BG/NBD, MBG/NBD, Pareto/NBD and shifted-BG classes; the BG/BB and Gamma-Gamma classes contain none. The Pareto/NBD covariates follow the time-invariant formulation; no time-varying or seasonal covariate code is present in the CLV models. The BG/BB class docstring requires that "all customers share the same value for T". — [pymc-marketing on PyPI (source of 1.4.0 inspected)](https://pypi.org/project/pymc-marketing/)
- [SN] PyMC-Marketing docs: default fit is NUTS with 4 chains and 1,000 draws; MAP fits use scipy L-BFGS-B. — [PyMC-Marketing BG/NBD notebook](https://pymc-marketing.readthedocs.io/en/stable/notebooks/clv/bg_nbd.html)
- [FT] Lifetimes 0.11.3 has fitters for BG/NBD, MBG/NBD, Pareto/NBD, Gamma-Gamma and BG/BB. — [Lifetimes on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/Lifetimes/)
- [FT] BTYD (R) and Lifetimes implement BG/BB; BTYDplus adds Pareto/GGG and hierarchical-Bayes Pareto/NBD. — [arXiv 2602.09845](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09845)

**Fitting cost at scale (published)**
- [FT] CLVTools Pareto/NBD estimation time, single core of an AMD Ryzen 9 9950X, L-BFGS-B, no Hessian, simulated data, median of 3 runs. For 1,000,000 customers: no covariate 1.23 s / 1.58 s / 2.33 s for 26 / 52 / 104 weeks of estimation period; one time-invariant covariate 2.07 s / 3.43 s / 5.60 s; one time-varying covariate 12 min 34 s / 34 min 22 s / 134 min 40 s. For 100,000 customers with a time-varying covariate: 1 min 30 s / 3 min 16 s / 10 min 39 s. Skipping the Hessian cuts run time about 20-30%; adding correlation between processes (Sarmanov) multiplies it by about 2.5 to 6. — [arXiv 2602.09845](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09845)
- [FT] The CLVTools authors state they are "aware of businesses with 10,000,000 customers that use this implementation of the Pareto/NBD model", and that applying probabilistic models "to millions of customers does not require specialized hardware such as graphics processing units". — [arXiv 2602.09845](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09845)
- [SN] A university case study using CLVTools reported that time-varying covariates made the algorithm computationally expensive and convergence sensitive to the optimiser, starting values and customer homogeneity. — [CMU Tepper retail-chain case study](https://www.cmu.edu/tepper/recruiting-and-partnerships/partner-with-the-tepper-school/assets/images/retail-chain.pdf)

### Inferences
- Scoring cost per user is a handful of special-function evaluations of (x, t_x, T) for the closed-form models: log-gamma and log-beta only for BG/BB, one 2F1 for BG/NBD, several 2F1 for Pareto/NBD. No published per-user scoring benchmark was found, so I measured BG/BB myself (below).
- Own measurement, this session (numpy/scipy, one core of a 4-core sandbox; script at `/tmp/claude-0/-home-user-lifetime-value-modeling/c1dd95e0-b66e-5048-a424-32abad8a3246/scratchpad/statclv/bin/bgbb_check.py`). In discrete time the BG/BB score depends only on the integer triple (x, t_x, n), so scoring is a table lookup. There are 1,379 distinct (x, t_x) cells at n = 52, and 2,963,480 distinct (x, t_x, n) cells for all n up to 260 weeks. Computing P(alive), expected active weeks over 52 weeks and P(any activity in 52 weeks) for all 2,963,480 cells took 171 s single-core; gathering the three scores for 10,000,000 users took 0.38 s. Direct (no table) vectorised scoring of 1,000,000 users at n = 156 took 52 s single-core. On this evidence a BG/BB reference baseline is far inside the 30-minute budget for 100M users; the binding cost is computing (x, t_x, n) per user, which the RFM feature pipeline for candidate A already produces.
- Own derivation, checked by simulation: for BG/BB, P(at least one active week in the next h weeks | x, t_x, n) = 1 - L(x, t_x, n+h) / L(x, t_x, n), where L is the BG/BB individual likelihood (the same history followed by h empty weeks). On 2,000,000 simulated BG/BB users (n = 52, h = 52) the closed forms matched simulation: mean P(alive) 0.13309 predicted vs 0.13316 simulated; mean expected future active weeks 3.38258 vs 3.37936; mean P(any) 0.13002 vs 0.13007; over the 102 cells with at least 2,000 users the largest absolute gaps were 0.0070 (P(alive)), 0.66 weeks (expected weeks) and 0.0072 (P(any)). The simulation parameters were arbitrary illustrative values, not estimates from any data set.
- Mapping to the five targets (my construction, not from a source): (1) P(any active week) = BG/BB horizon-return probability on weekly activity; (2) expected active weeks = BG/BB conditional expectation; (3) expected hours = expected active weeks times a Gamma-Gamma-style model of hours per active week; (4) P(spend > 0) = a second BG/BB on weekly spend incidence; (5) expected spend = expected spend weeks times Gamma-Gamma spend per spend week. Targets 3 and 5 inherit the frequency/value independence assumption that the ZILN and Glady et al. sources criticise.
- BG/NBD and MBG/NBD are a poor structural fit for weekly "active or not" data: they need continuous timing, and tying death to transactions makes heavy players "churn faster". BG/BB is the natural member of the family because its death opportunity is each period rather than each transaction.
- The season calendar is known in advance, which satisfies the CLVTools requirement that future covariate values be supplied. But the only closed-form time-varying-covariate model is continuous-time Pareto/NBD, and its published fit time (134 min for 1M customers at 104 weeks, single core) means it would have to be fitted on a sample and then used to score everyone.

### Gaps
- I could not open the Fader, Hardie, Shang (2010) paper itself; the BG/BB expressions above come from the Lifetimes source code and my own simulation check, not from the paper's text. The paper's empirical numbers (data set size, holdout fit) were not retrieved.
- No published benchmark of per-user scoring latency for any BTYD model was found.
- No BG/BB extension with covariates or seasonality was found in any package I inspected (PyMC-Marketing 1.4.0, Lifetimes 0.11.3) or paper I read; whether one exists in the journal literature is unknown.
- The Bachmann et al. (2021) accuracy gains from time-varying covariates were not available as numbers.
- The hardware for the CLVTools "10,000,000 customers" statement and the scoring (as opposed to fitting) time are not reported.

## 2. Which model fits a discrete weekly "active or not" panel, and can any represent lapse and return? BTYD versus the HMM-based CLV papers

### Takeaway
BG/BB is the BTYD member designed for discrete "opportunity" data, but like the whole family it treats death as permanent, so a long-dormant user who returns at a season launch is explained only as "was alive with a low activity rate all along". The HMM literature frames BTYD as a constrained special case (two states, absorbing death, no back-and-forth) and reports individual-level gains from allowing return, while BTYD-with-Gamma-Gamma often stays best on aggregate totals.

### Cited Findings
- [FT] "The BTYD tradition models a noncontractual customer as a latent two-process system: purchase while alive, die silently, never return." — [arXiv 2607.18623](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18623)
- [SN] "A subset of Hidden Markov Models are buy-till-you-die models, also known as latent attrition models, where a customer can be either active or churned and the churned state is absorbing." — [Customer-Base Analysis and Latent Attrition Models (online textbook chapter)](https://mike-marketing-research.share.connect.posit.cloud/13a-customer-base-analysis.html)
- [SN] Schwartz, Bradlow, Fader (2014, Marketing Science 33(2):188-205) treat the HMM and three constrained variants of it, including BG/BB, as one family for longitudinal incidence data; using 64 data sets they build a classification tree on easy-to-compute summary statistics that recommends which variant to use, and report that some model features, "such as back-and-forth migration between latent states", matter more than others for the choice. — [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:33:y:2014:i:2:p:188-205); [Knowledge at Wharton write-up](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/finding-the-right-tool-to-unlock-the-power-of-data/)
- [SN] Netzer, Lattin, Srinivasan (2008, Marketing Science 27(2):185-204): non-homogeneous HMM with hierarchical-Bayes MCMC on university alumni gift-giving; transitions among latent relationship states depend on customer-firm encounters; "superior predictive validity relative to a heterogeneous, yet static, latent class model and a dynamic and heterogeneous recency-frequency model"; a campaign was found to move alumni from the dormant state to an occasional-donation state. Sources differ on whether three or four states were used. — [INFORMS page](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.1070.0294); [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:27:y:2008:i:2:p:185-204)
- [SN] Schweidel, Bradlow, Fader (2011, Management Science 57(3):471-486): dynamic HMM whose latent states govern affinity for services in a multi-service portfolio, linking states to propensities to add services or terminate, and to CLV. This is a contractual multi-service setting. — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v57y2011i3p471-486.html)
- [SN] Romero, van der Lans, Wierenga (2013, Journal of Interactive Marketing 27(3):185-208): a partially hidden Markov model that relaxes the BTYD assumptions that customers keep one pattern and defect permanently, and lets customers become active again after temporary inactivity. Against four benchmarks, "at the individual level, it outperforms all benchmarks in predicting purchase frequency, total expenditure, and purchase incidence. At the aggregate level, the BG/NBD-GG and Pareto/NBD-GG models often perform best." — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/joinma/v27y2013i3p185-208.html); [Erasmus repository](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/41071)
- [SN] Ma & Büschken (2011, Marketing Letters 22(3):243-257), "Counting your customers from an 'always a share' perspective": a hidden-Markov (HIPP) model that "allows customers to switch from inactive to active states, which is not accommodated in traditional models like Pareto/NBD". — [Academia.edu copy](https://www.academia.edu/17334855/Counting_your_customers_from_an_always_a_share_perspective)
- [FT] HMM formulations "infer latent relationship states that simultaneously affect purchase incidence and intensity (e.g., Netzer et al., 2008)". — [arXiv 2608.02911](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02911)
- [FT] Within the beta-geometric family "alive" means "will purchase again eventually", so P(alive) is the infinite-horizon limit of the finite-horizon repeat-purchase probabilities and is only partially identified. On a seven-year panel of 31,683 customers, specifications with nearly identical observable forecasts gave "alive" counts from 3,654 to 27,734 (7.6x). At a 2021 scoring date with 60 months of follow-up, realised returners reached 313 by month 18 and 419 by month 60 and were still rising, which falsified the unpenalised maximum-likelihood alive counts of the MBG/NBD (213) and a spike model (207) from below. — [arXiv 2607.18623](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18623)
- [FT] Same study: summed MBG/NBD P(alive) overshot realised 18-month returners by 2.25x, while the same fitted model's own 18-month return forecast was off by 1.18x (expected calibration error 0.013, AUC 0.80). A static isotonic recalibration fitted on one fully observed scoring date and applied to the next brought every model within 1.06x to 1.13x; a dynamic cohort-based calibration layer gave aggregate bias 1.06x, 1.05x and 1.00x at 12, 18 and 24 months (expected calibration error 0.004, 0.007, 0.018). Native-forecast bias drifted upward with scoring-date recency (1.00, 1.10, 1.18 for 2022 to 2024). Single firm; the author co-founded the company that supplied the data. — [arXiv 2607.18623](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18623)
- [FT] Wübben and von Wangenheim (2008) validated Pareto/NBD activity classification against holdout purchase incidence "and found that a recency heuristic matched the model". — [arXiv 2607.18623](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18623)
- [FT] Dew & Ansari's Gaussian process propensity model was built on data from two mobile games and "generalizes hazard and buy-till-you-die models by incorporating calendar time dynamics while simultaneously accounting for recency and lifetime effects"; when its calendar-time components are muted, its fit resembles the BG/NBD fit. — [Dew & Ansari, arXiv 1511.05614](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.05614)

### Inferences
- A two-state HMM with an absorbing "dead" state and Beta-distributed heterogeneity is the BG/BB; candidate (B), the covariate-driven HMM with latent engagement states, therefore contains the BTYD story as a constrained case and adds exactly what the season calendar needs (non-absorbing dormant state, covariate-driven transitions). Adding BG/BB as a second "challenger" would duplicate (B) with stronger restrictions; its distinct value is as a near-free, closed-form reference.
- BG/BB can still assign non-zero return probability to long-dormant users, because dormancy is ambiguous between "dead" and "alive with low activity probability". What it cannot do is move that probability up at a season launch for everyone at once, or let a user be "re-acquired". Expect it to under-predict season-driven returns and to be miscalibrated around launches unless a calibration layer is added.
- For the target "P(any active week in the next 52 weeks)" the correct BTYD output is the horizon-return probability, not P(alive). The Ulrich results suggest the horizon forecast is reasonably calibrated out of the box and well calibrated after isotonic recalibration, but that calibration drifts across scoring dates, so recalibration must be refreshed.
- The Romero et al. pattern (HMM better individually, BTYD-GG better on aggregates) is a reason to score the bake-off at both levels.

### Gaps
- None of the three HMM papers could be read in full; I found no direct BG/BB-versus-HMM accuracy numbers, and the Schwartz et al. tree's actual splitting rules (when BG/BB is recommended over the full HMM) were not retrieved.
- No paper was found that tests BTYD models on game play-activity (weekly active/not) with season-driven returns. The game evidence found concerns purchases, not engagement.
- No evidence was found on how HMM-based CLV models scale beyond the small panels used in those papers.

## 3. Recent empirical head-to-heads (2019 to 2026): BTYD versus gradient boosting, RNN/LSTM, CNN and transformer models

### Takeaway
In every head-to-head I could read, a feature-rich or sequence model beat plain BTYD on individual-level error, often by a wide margin in consumer-app and game settings; but on sparse retail transaction data with no covariates Pareto/NBD plus Gamma-Gamma remains close to, and sometimes ahead of, an LSTM, and BTYD-type models are repeatedly reported as competitive on aggregate totals. No paper found compares BTYD with a modern transformer on individual-level LTV, and none uses a discrete weekly engagement panel.

### Cited Findings
- [FT] Meta Quest rolling LTV (user-level gross revenue; series up to 4 years; 100+ features), adjusted SMAPE at 4 / 13 / 26 weeks: BTYD 53.6% / 70.5% / 78.3%; LightGBM 30.4% / 53.7% / 61.1%; dilated RNN 22.9% / 39.8% / 51.2%. The BTYD variant and its inputs are not specified. In the same paper's Uber acquisition-LTV test (no BTYD), xgboost RMSE 2,292 beat a plain 2-cell RNN (2,830) and was narrowly beaten by the RNN with city embeddings (2,271). — [arXiv 2412.20295](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.20295)
- [FT] Free-to-play mobile game (Age of Ishtaria), 365-day LTV, 2,505 paying users. Prediction errors (RMSLE / NRMSE / SMAPE / % error): Pareto/NBD + average spend 9.35 / 1.88 / 95.65 / 8.96%; MBG/CNBD-k + average spend 3.46 / 1.68 / 75.53 / 7.96%; DNN 1.82 / 1.12 / 72.99 / 5.82%; CNN 1.84 / 1.05 / 73.76 / 5.72%. For the top 20% of spenders the % error was 33.35% (Pareto/NBD), 29.14% (MBG/CNBD-k), 15.76% (DNN), 15.64% (CNN). The parametric models "in many cases (wrongly) predict no purchases for players who actually keep on spending" and "systematically underestimate the expenditure of top-spending players". The authors note the deep models "make use of much more information". Regularity-aware BTYD variants were the best of the four parametric models tried. — [arXiv 1811.12799](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12799)
- [FT] Two mobile games, daily spend forecasts (holdout MAPE, Life Simulator / City Builder): BG/NBD 0.36 / 0.73; GPPM without calendar-time components 0.15 / 0.37; GPPM with only the cyclic calendar component 0.14 / 0.32; full GPPM 0.24 / 0.42; log-logistic hazard with covariates 0.47 / 0.49. The model was estimated with NUTS in Stan on 8,000 of 10,000 sampled customers, and the authors acknowledge "the data and computational demands are much steeper than for competing models". — [arXiv 1511.05614](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.05614)
- [FT] Three public retail data sets, no covariates, customer-level revenue RMSE at 52 / 104 / 156 / 208 weeks. Retailer A: Pareto/NBD+GG 119.59 / 219.75 / 336.24 / 459.58; LSTM+GG 105.58 / 173.67 / 262.14 / 359.41; CLVAE 102.00 / 165.09 / 256.23 / 352.40. Retailer B (three horizons): Pareto/NBD+GG 578.07 / 968.47 / 1,328.44; LSTM+GG 612.28 / 1,069.45 / 1,529.45; CLVAE 565.90 / 945.12 / 1,291.00. Retailer C: Pareto/NBD+GG 102.58 / 188.95 / 265.00 / 342.42; LSTM+GG 103.01 / 190.48 / 259.32 / 321.99; CLVAE 98.73 / 175.84 / 236.70 / 293.35. The LSTM is the Valendin et al. (2022) implementation with its recommended hyperparameters and automatically derived seasonality features. — [arXiv 2604.22636](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22636)
- [FT] Same paper, Retailer C with monthly acquisition-cohort covariates (RMSE at 52 / 104 / 156 / 208 weeks): Pareto/NBD with covariates + GG 104.75 / 192.59 / 270.69 / 350.09 (worse than without covariates); separate Pareto/NBD + GG per cohort 103.75 / 190.79 / 265.79 / 338.17; LSTM with covariates + GG 102.36 / 184.00 / 248.87 / 307.31; CLVAE with covariates 98.32 / 173.74 / 233.50 / 284.11. — [arXiv 2604.22636](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22636)
- [SN] Valendin, Reutterer, Platzer, Kalcher (2022, IJRM 39(4):988-1018): across eight empirical scenarios the LSTM's aggregate forecast deviates from actuals by 2.8% on average, against an average bias of 11.8% (Pareto/NBD), 13.2% (Pareto/GGG) and 19.2% (GPPM); individual-level MAPE is reduced by 44% on average relative to the next-best benchmark (Pareto/GGG), most in the more seasonal settings; the base LSTM is on average 57% better at spotting "opportunity customers" than the best benchmark. — [ScienceDirect page](https://sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167811622000180); [ResearchGate copy](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/359177537_Customer_Base_Analysis_with_Recurrent_Neural_Networks)
- [FT] Cohort-level weekly forecasting for 966 companies in 25 industries (card-panel data, 2016 to 2020), mean SMAPE. Acquisition: multi-task transformer (CBMT) 19.30; Weibull-Gamma + Pareto/NBD with covariates (WG-PNBD-E) 23.31; LSTM 26.03; random forest 26.02. Repeat orders per customer: CBMT 35.56; random forest 37.26; LSTM 42.56; WG-PNBD-E 50.85. Total-sales error for CBMT is about 30% below the strongest established customer-base benchmark; CBMT has the lowest mean SMAPE in 24 of 25 industries, with WG-PNBD-E lower only in Charitable Giving. CBMT's reduction relative to WG-PNBD-E narrows from 27.7% in the easiest tercile of firms to 12.0% in the hardest. This is an aggregate (cohort) comparison, not individual-level. — [arXiv 2608.02911](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02911)
- [FT] E-commerce, about 2 million customers, 6-month holdout: "% accuracy on hold-out period" 88.6% for the statistical model versus 94.6% for a fully connected network on seven summary features. The accuracy metric is not defined, and the paper calls the statistical model Pareto/GGG while saying it was implemented with the Lifetimes library. — [arXiv 2102.05771](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.05771)
- [FT] UCI Online Retail, number of transactions in the next 3 months (RMSE / MAE): BG/NBD 1.62 / 0.90; LightGBM 1.95 / 0.88; DNN 1.53 / 0.83; random forest 1.44 / 0.87; XGBoost 1.34 / 0.83; stacked regressor 1.37 / 0.82. LightGBM was worse than BG/NBD on RMSE here. — [arXiv 2308.08502](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08502)
- [SN] Bauer & Jannach (2021, ACM TKDD): encoder-decoder RNN with temporal convolutions, stacked with a GBM, on a large e-commerce company and a public online-retail data set; reported to outperform all baselines, with the stack further improving accuracy. Which BTYD baselines were included and their numbers were not retrievable. — [Author PDF listing](https://web-ainf.aau.at/pub/jannach/files/Journal_TKDD_2021.pdf)
- [SN] Simon & Adler (2022, Journal of Business Economics 92(4):707-733) and a 2025 follow-up compare Pareto/NBD estimated by MCMC, maximum likelihood and heuristics; MCMC "yields slightly better results than MLE" and adds intervals for future purchases. — [Springer article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11573-021-01057-6); [IDEAS record of the 2025 paper](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/jbecon/v95y2025i8d10.1007_s11573-025-01237-8.html)
- [SN, low reliability] A public GitHub project on the Olist e-commerce data reports XGBoost with the lowest MAE (5.71) and BG/NBD + Gamma-Gamma with the lowest RMSE (36.31). Single small data set, not peer reviewed. — [GitHub project](https://github.com/irenemarralii/Customer-Lifetime-Value-Prediction-Project)
- [FT] Stated reasons BTYD loses in app settings: independence of amount, frequency and active duration "rarely holds"; seasonality and other features are hard to add; new users have too little history to discriminate; multiple lines of business must be modelled separately. — [arXiv 2412.20295](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.20295)
- [FT] CLVAE authors' view of when sequence models pay off: LSTM or transformer encoders help when behaviour has "meaningful temporal structure or additional irregularity that is not accounted for by the RFM data"; their own LSTM-encoder variant beat the RFM-input variant on one of three retailers and was "somewhat worse in the other two". — [arXiv 2604.22636](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22636)

### Inferences
- The size of the BTYD deficit tracks how much signal lies outside (x, t_x, T). It is large where rich behavioural features and calendar effects exist (Meta Quest, games) and small or reversed on sparse, covariate-free retail histories (Retailer B and C). The planned product (hours, spend, platform, country, season calendar, dense weekly activity for engaged users) is in the first group, so a BTYD model should be expected to lose on individual-level metrics to candidates A and C.
- Results on aggregates are more favourable to BTYD: Romero et al. report BTYD-GG often best at the aggregate level, and in the CBMT study the probabilistic benchmark beat LSTM and random forest on acquisition while losing on repeat orders. Valendin et al. report the opposite for aggregate bias (LSTM 2.8% vs 11.8% for Pareto/NBD), so aggregate superiority of BTYD is not a safe assumption, especially with seasonality.
- The two game studies agree on the specific failure modes to check in the bake-off: under-prediction for top spenders, false "no future purchases" for continuing spenders, and missing calendar effects (holiday or content updates).
- In the one direct test of static covariates inside Pareto/NBD (CLVAE Table 3), cohort covariates made Pareto/NBD slightly worse, so "BTYD plus covariates" should not be assumed to close the gap.

### Gaps
- I could not read Valendin et al. (2022) or Bauer & Jannach (2021) in full; their numbers above are from search snippets.
- No 2023 to 2026 paper was found that compares BTYD with a transformer on individual-level LTV, or any BTYD-versus-GBDT comparison at tens of millions of users.
- No head-to-head was found that uses BG/BB (discrete time) as the statistical baseline, or that evaluates engagement targets such as active weeks or hours.
- Calibration is almost never reported in these comparisons; the only calibration evidence found is the Ulrich paper in section 2.
- Google's lifetime_value work (the ZILN paper) contains no BTYD baseline; see section 5.

## 4. Hierarchical Bayesian and neural-statistical hybrids: what exists, and does any scale to 100M users?

### Takeaway
Two 2026 preprints put BTYD likelihoods inside amortized variational inference: CLVAE (Pareto/NBD plus Gamma-Gamma with a neural mixing distribution) and "Wei' 'Til You Die" (Weibull-renewal BTYD fitted to 5 million customers in 8 minutes). They beat or match their statistical parents and, in CLVAE's case, an LSTM benchmark, but the largest demonstrated scale is 5 million customers; nothing I found shows a BTYD-type or hierarchical Bayesian model at 100M users, and MCMC-based models (HB Pareto/NBD, Pareto/GGG, GPPM, HMMs) are demonstrated only on thousands to tens of thousands of customers.

### Cited Findings
- [FT] WTYD (Ghosh & Schein, University of Chicago, August 2026): Weibull renewal transactions with customer-level regularity, frequency and churn latents; amortized variational inference with a recognition network, reparameterisation gradients, Rao-Blackwellisation and minibatches; reference implementation in PyTorch. "The proposed model fits to a proprietary dataset of 5 million online retail customers in 8 minutes which would take the current state-of-the-art an estimated 3-4 days" (the comparison is Pareto/GGG with MCMC, extrapolated; MCMC was actually run only on subsets of 1,000, 2,500 and 10,000 customers). A second case study covers 4 million political donors with covariates. — [arXiv 2608.19022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19022)
- [FT] WTYD accuracy: on real-data subsets both models reach comparable held-out posterior predictive density, "with WTYD further exhibiting substantially lower mean-squared error in its approximate frequency forecasts"; at n = 10,000 WTYD converges before the Pareto/GGG chain has burned in. Covariates improved held-out log-likelihood for only about two-thirds of test donors. — [arXiv 2608.19022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19022)
- [FT] WTYD trade-offs: it loses the sufficient-statistic representation (the recognition network consumes each customer's full list of inter-transaction times, summarised into a fixed vector of "pseudo-sufficient" power sums, P = 25); expected lifetime value is not analytic (Monte Carlo over latents plus a Weibull-count recurrence, or a renewal-theorem approximation); P(alive) has a closed-form approximation from the variational posterior. — [arXiv 2608.19022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19022)
- [FT] The WTYD paper's own comparison table: Pareto/NBD and (M)BG/NBD have sufficient statistics, covariates and scalable MLE but Poisson timing; Pareto/GGG has flexible timing but MCMC and no covariates; (M)BG/CNBD-k is scalable but with homogeneous, non-clumpy regularity; CLVAE is scalable VI with Poisson timing; WTYD is scalable VI with flexible timing but no sufficient statistics. — [arXiv 2608.19022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19022)
- [FT] CLVAE (Näf, Mbelson, Meierer, April 2026): keeps the Pareto/NBD + Gamma-Gamma process likelihood conditional on customer latents and replaces the parametric Gamma mixing distributions with an encoder-decoder trained by amortized variational inference; base inputs are only (x, t_x, T, mean spend); one model for attrition, transactions and spend; PyTorch; same hyperparameters across data sets. It had the lowest RMSE in every data set and horizon of the paper's Table 2 (numbers in section 3). Time-varying covariates are not yet supported. — [arXiv 2604.22636](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22636)
- [FT] "Alongside a recent preprint by Näf et al. (2026), this paper is among the first to adapt amortized VI to BTYDs." — [arXiv 2608.19022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19022)
- [FT] Xie (2019): a neural network that estimates Pareto/NBD parameters for out-of-sample customers with the Pareto/NBD likelihood embedded in the loss; the abstract reports that plain Pareto/NBD "underestimates repeat purchases at both the individual and aggregate levels" while the likelihood-in-loss network predicts repeat purchases well at both levels and is "highly efficient". Single-author preprint; I read only the abstract and introduction. — [arXiv 1911.01919](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.01919)
- [FT] Gaussian process propensity model (Dew & Ansari): Bayesian nonparametric model with latent functions over calendar time, inter-purchase time and lifetime; fitted with NUTS in Stan on 8,000 customers; authors note steep data and computational demands. — [arXiv 1511.05614](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.05614)
- [SN] Padilla & Ascarza (2021, JMR), a probabilistic machine-learning model for the cold-start problem using acquisition characteristics: described as applicable to large customer counts, but its illustrative example used 2,000 customers for estimation and 200 for evaluation. — [SAGE article page](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00222437211032938)
- [FT] Hierarchical-Bayes Pareto/NBD (Abe 2009) and Pareto/GGG are fitted by MCMC in BTYDplus. — [arXiv 2602.09845](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09845); [arXiv 2608.19022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19022)
- [FT] Joint attrition-transaction-spend models with correlated processes (Glady et al. 2015 and an improved version used as a benchmark) were "often comparable to, or worse than" the Pareto/NBD + GG baseline, and the CLVAE authors say such joint models' "computational complexity and limited improvements in predictive performance have prevented their adoption by practitioners". — [arXiv 2604.22636](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22636)
- [FT] The ZILN deep probabilistic model (Google) is a neural-statistical hybrid of a different kind: a DNN outputs the parameters of a zero-inflated lognormal for LTV, giving a predictive distribution without modelling churn or repeat purchase dynamics. — [arXiv 1912.07753](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.07753)

### Inferences
- Largest scale actually demonstrated for a BTYD-type model in what I read: 5 million customers (WTYD, fit time 8 minutes; hardware not stated in the passages I read). Amortized VI trains on minibatches and scores with one network forward pass plus closed-form or short Monte Carlo steps, so 100M-user scoring is plausible in principle, but it is undemonstrated and both models are continuous-time purchase models that would need adaptation to a discrete weekly panel.
- Classic closed-form BTYD is the one statistical family whose scoring trivially meets the 30-minute constraint (section 1): parameters can be fitted on a sample or on the recency-frequency count table and applied to everyone.
- The two VI papers are evidence that "BTYD structure plus neural heterogeneity" is the current research frontier in marketing science. They are closer in spirit to candidate C with a structured likelihood head than to a separate candidate; neither supports time-varying covariates such as a season calendar today.
- MCMC-based hierarchical models (HB Pareto/NBD, Pareto/GGG, GPPM, hierarchical-Bayes HMMs) are not viable for weekly refits on 100M users on the evidence found; they could only be fitted on samples.

### Gaps
- Hardware used for the WTYD 8-minute fit, and any per-user scoring time for WTYD or CLVAE, were not found in the passages read.
- No "deep survival" or neural BTYD model evaluated on a discrete weekly engagement panel was found, and no hierarchical Bayesian CLV model fitted with variational inference at 100M-user scale.
- Both 2026 VI papers are preprints (CLVAE is under anonymous review per its text); no independent replication was found.
- I did not find the WTYD or CLVAE code repositories (the CLVAE link is removed for review).

## 5. Is BTYD commonly used as a reference baseline in recent LTV papers, and how does it do?

### Takeaway
Usage splits by community. Marketing-science papers still use Pareto/NBD (+ Gamma-Gamma) as the standard benchmark and it is usually beaten but not embarrassed; industrial deep-LTV papers (Google, Kuaishou, Baidu, mobile games) cite BTYD in related work and then benchmark only against other ML models. Where an industry paper does include BTYD, it is the weakest row by a wide margin.

### Cited Findings
- [FT] Marketing-science usage: CLVAE benchmarks against Pareto/NBD + GG, described as "likely the most used modeling approach in industry"; WTYD benchmarks against Pareto/GGG; the 966-company CBMT study includes Pareto/NBD-based benchmarks with and without covariates among 15 benchmark variants. — [arXiv 2604.22636](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22636); [arXiv 2608.19022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19022); [arXiv 2608.02911](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02911)
- [FT] Industry paper with a BTYD row: Meta Quest rolling LTV, where BTYD is last at every horizon (aSMAPE 53.6% / 70.5% / 78.3% versus 30.4% / 53.7% / 61.1% for LightGBM). — [arXiv 2412.20295](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.20295)
- [FT] ZILN (Google) discusses BTYD only as background and does not benchmark it, on the grounds that "the BTYD model family does not apply to new customers because it uses frequency and recency to differentiate customers. New customers, however, have identical purchase frequency and recency." It also cites Gupta et al. (2006) for evidence that ML methods such as random forests outperform RFM and BTYD because they use more features. — [arXiv 1912.07753](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.07753)
- [FT] Kuaishou's billion-user LTV paper mentions the BTYD family only in related work. — [arXiv 2208.13358](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.13358)
- [FT] A 2025 mobile-gamer LTV paper mentions BTYD and BG/NBD only in related work, stating that "the BTYD family models have limitations in LTV prediction as the models focus on predicting consumption habits instead of the spending amount". — [arXiv 2510.08281](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.08281)
- [FT] AgentLTV (2026) places probabilistic models in a first "stream" whose distributional assumptions "may simplify one's behaviors and thus reduce the model accuracy", and says BTYD models "offer strong interpretability" but "are limited in modeling long-term dynamics and fine-grained monetary outcomes"; BTYD appears only in its literature review. — [arXiv 2602.21634](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21634)
- [FT] Baidu's HT-GNN paper (2026) contains no BTYD terms at all in its text; [SN] its baselines are XGBoost, ZILN, MDME, expLTV and HST-GT. — [arXiv 2601.13013](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.13013)
- [FT] ASOS's deployed CLTV system paper reviews BTYD under "distribution fitting approaches" in related work (I checked only that section). — [arXiv 1703.02596](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02596)
- [FT] Burelli's survey of LTV prediction in free-to-play games presents Pareto/NBD, BG/NBD and Gamma-Gamma (via Lifetimes and BTYD) as standard tools, and names their reliance on "a small number of purely transactional data" as both their main strength (generality) and main limitation (the model "will always risk to be sub-optimal as it disregards potentially relevant information"). — [arXiv 2209.12619](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.12619)
- [FT] Small-scale ML papers still use BG/NBD as "the baseline": on UCI Online Retail it had RMSE 1.62 / MAE 0.90 against 1.34 to 1.95 / 0.82 to 0.88 for five ML models. — [arXiv 2308.08502](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08502)
- [FT] Advantages claimed for probabilistic models by their maintainers: no need to split data to create a label (all data used for estimation), three-column data requirement, reliable long-horizon extrapolation even when the horizon exceeds the estimation period, scalability to millions without GPUs, and interpretable covariate effects. — [arXiv 2602.09845](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09845)
- [FT] One practitioner's summary after an e-commerce comparison: if explainability, "probability alive" or fast processing matter, use the statistical model; for the highest accuracy, use the neural network. — [arXiv 2102.05771](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.05771)

### Inferences
- For the decision at hand: the evidence supports adding BG/BB (with a Gamma-Gamma-style value model) as a **reference baseline**, not as a production **candidate**. Reasons: (a) it is nearly free to fit and score at 100M users; (b) it gives a structural, label-free long-horizon extrapolation against which leakage or horizon problems in A and C show up; (c) it produces P(alive)-type outputs stakeholders recognise; but (d) every readable head-to-head in feature-rich consumer-app or game settings has it losing clearly on individual-level accuracy, it has no mechanism for season-driven returns, and it cannot use platform or country except by fitting separate segment models.
- If a statistical model is wanted as a real candidate, the covariate HMM already in the plan (B) is the stronger choice, since it nests the BTYD structure and relaxes the two assumptions that matter here (permanent death, no calendar time). The 2026 amortized-VI BTYD models are worth tracking but are unproven beyond 5 million users and lack time-varying covariates.
- A BG/BB baseline should be scored on its horizon outputs (probability of any active week in 52 weeks, expected active weeks), with an isotonic recalibration step, and should be reported at both aggregate (cohort total) and individual levels, because BTYD's relative standing differs between the two.
- Because industrial deep-LTV papers rarely include BTYD, the claim that "deep models beat BTYD at scale" rests on few direct tests (Meta Quest; small game samples). The bake-off itself would be one of the larger direct comparisons.

### Gaps
- I did not systematically count baseline usage across the LTV literature; the split described above rests on the roughly fifteen papers read here.
- No paper was found reporting how a BTYD baseline performs on engagement targets (active weeks, hours) as opposed to purchases.
- No source quantifies the cost of segment-wise BG/BB (per platform and country) as a substitute for covariates.
